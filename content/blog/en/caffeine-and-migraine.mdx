You reach for a coffee when a migraine starts, and sometimes it seems to help. Other times you skip your morning cup on a lazy Saturday and end up with a pounding head by lunchtime. So what is the real link between caffeine and migraine: does coffee help or hurt?

The honest answer about **caffeine and migraine** is that it can do both, depending on how much you take, how regularly you take it and how your own brain reacts. This article explains why, where caffeine hides in your day, and how to test your own reaction without guesswork. Caffeine is one of several [migraine triggers](/blog/migraine-triggers) worth understanding, but it is a more nuanced one than most.

**In short**

- Caffeine can help ease a migraine for some people, but it is also more often a risk factor than a treatment, according to the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/caffeine-and-migraine/).
- In a 2019 Harvard-led diary study, one or two caffeinated drinks a day were not linked to more headaches, but three or more were, according to [Harvard Medical School](https://hms.harvard.edu/news/twos-my-limit).
- Skipping your usual caffeine can trigger an attack: in a small randomized trial, [sudden withdrawal triggered severe migraine in seven of nine people](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33013662/) who drank 300 to 800 mg a day.
- Caffeine hides in tea, cola, energy drinks and some painkillers, so tracking your real intake and your attacks for a few weeks is the best way to find your own pattern.

## Why can caffeine both help and trigger a migraine?

Caffeine plays on both sides because it changes how your brain's blood vessels and chemical messengers behave, and the effect depends on the dose and your habits. A 2020 review in the journal Nutrients, ["The Ambiguous Role of Caffeine in Migraine Headache: From Trigger to Treatment"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7468766), describes caffeine as blocking adenosine receptors. Adenosine is a natural substance in the brain that, among other things, affects blood vessel width.

The same review notes that caffeine or caffeine withdrawal was found to be a migraine trigger in a small share of participants across 17 studies (from 2% to 30%), while all the treatment studies found it safe and effective for acute migraine. That wide range tells you something important: this is very individual. Some people are clearly sensitive, many are not.

One more nuance: the exact mechanism is still debated. The review mentions that caffeine can narrow blood vessels in the brain, and that stopping it can lead to a rebound widening that may contribute to caffeine withdrawal headache. Migraine itself is not simply a blood vessel problem, though. If you want the bigger picture, see [what causes migraines](/blog/what-causes-migraines).

## How can caffeine ease a migraine attack?

Caffeine can help some people during an attack, mostly in small amounts and when it is not used every day. The [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/does-caffeine-cause-migraines) says caffeine can help with migraine and tension headaches, and that roughly 100 to 150 mg (about a cup of coffee) may help, while too much can cause pain.

You may already be taking caffeine without realizing it. The American Migraine Foundation notes that [many over-the-counter headache and migraine medicines contain caffeine](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/caffeine-and-migraine/), sometimes because it helps the body absorb the other ingredients. To compare common painkillers, see [painkiller for headache](/blog/painkiller-for-headache).

There is a catch. The same foundation says that treating headaches with caffeine is sometimes effective but should be limited to no more than two days per week. Using it more often can lead to dependence and more frequent attacks. The same logic applies to painkillers in general, as explained in [medication overuse headache](/blog/medication-overuse-headache).

Whether caffeine is right for you during an attack is a question for your doctor or pharmacist, especially if you already use other migraine medication.

## When does coffee become a trigger?

Coffee tends to become a trigger when you drink more than your usual amount, or when your intake swings from day to day. The American Migraine Foundation says that for people with migraine, caffeine taken [three or more days per week may lead to dependency and increased migraine frequency](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/caffeine-and-migraine/).

The most useful study here is a prospective cohort study in The American Journal of Medicine. Participants with episodic migraine filled in diaries twice a day for six weeks. [Harvard Medical School](https://hms.harvard.edu/news/twos-my-limit) summarizes the findings:

- One to two servings of caffeinated drinks a day were not linked to a higher chance of a headache that day.
- Three or more servings were linked to higher odds of a migraine headache that day.
- Among people who rarely drank caffeine, even one or two servings were linked to higher odds.

Note what this shows and what it doesn't. It shows an association in people who already had migraine, and it held after accounting for sleep, stress, alcohol and periods. It does not prove that coffee caused each attack, and it can't tell you your personal threshold.

If you have severe or frequent attacks, the American Migraine Foundation says doctors may suggest [cutting caffeine out for a few months](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/caffeine-and-migraine/) and then reintroducing it carefully. That is a decision to make with your doctor, not on your own in the middle of a bad month.

## Why can missing your coffee give you a headache at the weekend?

Missing your usual caffeine can trigger a headache because your body gets used to a daily dose and reacts when it drops. The [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/does-caffeine-cause-migraines) says that if you regularly consume caffeine and then go a day or two without it, you can have caffeine withdrawal, and migraine can be one of the symptoms.

A small randomized, double-blind trial from Norway tested this directly. It enrolled people with episodic migraine who drank 300 to 800 mg of caffeine daily. The study was planned for 80 people but stopped early because too few signed up, so only ten took part. In that small group, [sudden withdrawal triggered severe migraine attacks in seven of nine people](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33013662/), while continuing caffeine did not. With so few participants, treat it as a strong signal rather than a final answer.

This may be part of the puzzle behind [weekend migraines](/blog/weekend-migraines). On a work day you have your usual coffee at 8:00. On Saturday you sleep in, have your first cup at noon, or skip it entirely, and your body notices. A later wake-up can also upset your rhythm, which is covered in [migraine and sleep](/blog/migraine-and-sleep).

The [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/nutrition-and-healthy-eating/in-depth/caffeine/art-20045678) says withdrawal symptoms can include headache, tiredness, crankiness and trouble focusing, and that they are usually mild and ease after a few days. If you want to cut back, it suggests doing it little by little, for example one fewer can of soda or a smaller cup each day.

## Where is caffeine hiding besides coffee?

Caffeine is in more than your coffee mug: tea, cola, energy drinks and energy shots all count, and some painkillers too. Most people underestimate their daily total, which matters because both too much and sudden changes can play a role.

The [Mayo Clinic's caffeine chart](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/nutrition-and-healthy-eating/in-depth/caffeine/art-20049372) gives these approximate amounts:

| Drink | Approximate caffeine |
|---|---|
| Brewed coffee (8 oz) | 96 mg |
| Espresso (1 oz) | 63 mg |
| Instant coffee (8 oz) | 62 mg |
| Brewed black tea (8 oz) | 48 mg |
| Brewed green tea (8 oz) | 29 mg |
| Energy drink (8 oz) | 79 mg |
| Energy shot (2 oz) | 200 mg |

A few things to keep in mind:

- **Café sizes are bigger than 8 oz.** A large takeaway coffee can easily count as two or more servings. Amounts also vary by brand and brewing method, so treat the table as a rough guide.
- **Energy drinks and shots can add up fast.** A single 2 oz shot has about the same caffeine as two cups of brewed coffee, according to the Mayo Clinic figures above.
- **Tea is not caffeine-free.** Black tea has roughly half the caffeine of coffee per cup, so three or four cups still add up.
- **Medicines may contain it.** Check the label of any headache or cold remedy, and ask your pharmacist if you are unsure.

The Mayo Clinic says up to [400 mg a day seems safe for most adults](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/nutrition-and-healthy-eating/in-depth/caffeine/art-20045678), which is about four cups of brewed coffee. That is a general safety figure, not a migraine target. For people with migraine, the American Migraine Foundation says doctors usually suggest [one or two caffeinated drinks a day, or about 200 mg](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/caffeine-and-migraine/), for episodic migraine.

Caffeine is only one part of what you eat and drink. For the wider picture, see [migraine and food](/blog/migraine-and-food).

## How can you test your own reaction to caffeine?

The most reliable way is to write down what you drink and when your attacks happen, for at least a few weeks, then look for a pattern. Memory is unreliable here: we tend to remember the coffee before a bad day and forget the many coffees before good days. For a general method, see [how to identify your migraine triggers](/blog/identify-migraine-triggers).

Here is a simple approach:

1. **Track your normal week first.** Don't change anything yet. Note every caffeinated drink, roughly its size and the time you had it, including tea, cola and energy drinks.
2. **Record each attack.** Note when it started, how strong it was and how long it lasted. Our guide to [what to write down in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) covers the basics.
3. **Add the other suspects.** Sleep, stress, your cycle and skipped meals can all matter, and the Harvard study accounted for them for a reason. A trigger rarely acts alone.
4. **Look for three patterns.** Do attacks follow days with more caffeine than usual? Do they follow days with less, such as the weekend? Or is there no link at all?
5. **Bring the results to your doctor.** If you see a pattern, show them your history before changing anything. A [migraine report for your doctor](/blog/migraine-report-for-doctor) makes that conversation easier.

Be careful about changing several things at once. If you cut caffeine, move your bedtime and start a new supplement in the same fortnight, you won't know which change did what.

## Do you have to give up coffee if you have migraines?

Not necessarily: many people with migraine can keep a modest, steady amount of caffeine, and the evidence doesn't support a blanket ban. The [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/does-caffeine-cause-migraines) says that one or two servings a day may even help prevent migraines, while more than that can trigger one, so steady and moderate is the common thread.

What seems to matter most is consistency. That means a similar amount, at a similar time, including at weekends, and not suddenly doubling up during a stressful week. If you want to cut down, do it gradually, as the Mayo Clinic suggests above, rather than going cold turkey.

There are cases where you should talk to a professional first:

- You have frequent attacks, or a headache on many days of the month (see [why you might have a headache every day](/blog/headache-every-day)).
- You use painkillers often, including ones that contain caffeine.
- You are pregnant or trying to conceive. The [NHS advises limiting caffeine to 200 mg a day](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/) in pregnancy, and your midwife or doctor can guide you.
- Your headaches have changed or feel different from usual.

If you're ever unsure whether a sudden headache needs urgent care, read [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine).

## Conclusion: neither enemy nor friend

Caffeine is neither a villain nor a cure for migraine. A modest, regular amount seems fine for many people, a coffee may help some people during an attack, and big or irregular amounts, including a missed morning cup, can tip others into one.

The useful question is not "Is coffee bad for migraine?" but "What does caffeine do for me?" A few weeks of notes on your drinks, your sleep and your attacks will usually tell you more than any general rule. And if your pattern is unclear or your attacks are frequent, bring your notes to your doctor.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does coffee help a migraine?

It can for some people. The Cleveland Clinic says caffeine, about 100 to 150 mg, can help with migraine pain, and many over-the-counter headache medicines contain it. The American Migraine Foundation, however, advises limiting caffeine for headaches to no more than two days a week and says it is more often a risk factor than a treatment.

### How much caffeine is too much with migraine?

There is no single number that fits everyone. In a Harvard-led diary study, three or more caffeinated drinks a day were linked to more migraine headaches, while one or two were not. Your own threshold may be lower or higher, which is why tracking is useful.

### Why do I get a headache when I skip my morning coffee?

Your body adjusts to a regular caffeine dose, and a sudden drop can cause withdrawal, including headache. The Mayo Clinic says this usually eases within a few days. If you often wake up with a headache, also look at your sleep, as explained in [migraine and sleep](/blog/migraine-and-sleep).

### Are decaf coffee and tea safe if I have migraine?

Decaf has much less caffeine than regular coffee, but it is not always completely caffeine-free, so it can still matter if you are very sensitive. Coffee and tea contain other compounds too, and individual reactions differ. Your diary will show whether decaf makes any difference for you.

### Can energy drinks trigger migraines?

They can add a lot of caffeine quickly. The Mayo Clinic lists about 79 mg in an 8 oz energy drink and about 200 mg in a 2 oz energy shot, so a couple of cans plus your usual coffee can push you well past two or three servings. If you suspect they affect you, note them separately in your diary.
