You have an attack every week or two. You keep reaching for your pain relief, you cancel plans, and you start wondering whether **migraine prevention medication** could help you have fewer attacks.

There is, and it is called **migraine prevention medication** (also called preventive or prophylactic treatment). This article gives you an overview of when doctors suggest it, the main families of drugs (leaving aside [CGRP treatments](/blog/cgrp-migraine-treatment) and [Botox](/blog/botox-for-chronic-migraine), which have their own guides), what you can realistically expect, and what to ask at your appointment. For the bigger picture of all options, you can also visit our [migraine treatments guide](/blog/migraine-treatments).

This is general information, not advice about what you should take. Choosing a preventive is always a decision between you and your doctor.

**In short**

- Doctors usually discuss a preventive when attacks are frequent, very disabling, or when pain relief is not working well or is being used too often.
- The main oral families are beta blockers, some antidepressants, some blood pressure medicines (such as candesartan) and some anti-seizure drugs (such as topiramate).
- Valproate and topiramate carry serious pregnancy risks, so special safeguards apply to women who could become pregnant.
- A preventive usually needs about two to three months to judge, and success is usually defined as roughly half as many migraine days, not zero.

## When do doctors suggest migraine prevention medication?

Doctors usually suggest a preventive when attacks are frequent or severely disrupt your life, not just when you have a certain number of headaches. The American Migraine Foundation says [a preventive is ideal if you have more than four migraine attacks a month or very disabling attacks](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-prevention-101/). The American Headache Society's consensus statement also takes into account both how many headache days you have and [how much disability they cause](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34160823/), so there is no single magic number.

Other situations can tip the balance too. A preventive may be discussed if pain relief does not work well, if you cannot take it safely, if you need a long time to recover after each attack, or if you are taking acute medicines so often that [medication overuse headache](/blog/medication-overuse-headache) becomes a risk. If your attacks drag on for days, our guide to a [migraine that won't go away](/blog/migraine-that-wont-go-away) explains when to get help.

In the UK, the NHS says [preventive medicines are usually used if you have tried avoiding possible triggers but still get migraines](https://nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/prevention). Your GP can often start one, and may refer you to a neurologist if it does not go well.

It also helps to know where you sit on the spectrum. Our article on [chronic vs episodic migraine](/blog/chronic-migraine-vs-episodic-migraine) explains the 15-days-a-month line, which can influence which options are considered.

## How is prevention different from the pills you take during an attack?

Pain relief and [triptans](/blog/triptans-explained) are taken during an attack to stop it, while preventives are taken regularly to make attacks less frequent or milder. The Mayo Clinic explains that [preventive medicines are taken regularly, often daily, and can reduce how often you get a migraine, how painful attacks are and how long they last](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207).

Most people with a preventive still keep an acute treatment for the attacks that get through. If you want a refresher on those, see our overview of [migraine medication](/blog/migraine-medication).

## The main families of migraine prevention medication

Several different drug families are used to prevent migraine, and most of them were first developed for something else, such as high blood pressure, depression or epilepsy. The Mayo Clinic groups them into [blood pressure medicines, antidepressants and anti-seizure medicines](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207). That does not mean you have those conditions. These drugs also happen to calm the brain's migraine activity, though researchers do not fully understand how.

### Beta blockers: a classic first choice

Beta blockers such as propranolol and metoprolol are among the best-known preventives. The Mayo Clinic lists [propranolol and metoprolol](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207) as examples of cardiovascular drugs used for migraine prevention.

Possible side effects include tiredness, low mood, nausea, trouble sleeping and dizziness, according to the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/understanding-migrainepreventive-treatments/). The Migraine Trust notes that [beta blockers should not be taken if you have asthma or COPD](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/healthcare/treatments/preventive-medicines/), which is one reason your doctor will ask about your health history first.

### Antidepressants: amitriptyline and others

Tricyclic antidepressants such as amitriptyline and nortriptyline are among the most commonly used migraine preventives. The Mayo Clinic says [they are the most commonly used medicines for prevention](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207), and that another antidepressant with fewer side effects or interactions, such as venlafaxine, might be prescribed instead.

Taking an antidepressant for migraine does not mean your doctor thinks your migraines are "in your head". The doses used and the reason for using them can be quite different from treating depression, and your doctor can explain that to you.

### Blood pressure medicines other than beta blockers

Candesartan and verapamil are two other options from the blood pressure family. The American Migraine Foundation lists [beta blockers, calcium channel blockers and candesartan](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-prevention-101/) as antihypertensive drugs used for migraine prevention. The Mayo Clinic adds that verapamil [can be helpful in preventing migraines with aura](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207).

For candesartan, a small crossover trial of 72 people found [43% of participants had at least a 50% drop in attacks on candesartan, compared with 23% on placebo](https://www.pubmed.gov/24335848/). It is a modest trial, so it points in a helpful direction but is not the last word. The Migraine Trust warns that candesartan is [not recommended in pregnancy or when planning a pregnancy](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/healthcare/treatments/preventive-medicines/).

### Anti-seizure medicines: topiramate and valproate

Topiramate and valproate were first developed for epilepsy. The NHS says topiramate [has been shown to help prevent migraines and is usually taken daily as a tablet](https://nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/prevention). Side effects can include tingling, trouble finding words, memory problems and reduced appetite, as described by the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-prevention-101/).

These two drugs need special care for women, which is covered in the next section.

## Why valproate and topiramate need extra care for women

Both valproate and topiramate can seriously harm an unborn baby, so women who could become pregnant are offered them only with strict safeguards, or not at all. Since most readers of this blog are women of childbearing age, this part matters.

**Valproate.** In the US, the FDA says valproate is contraindicated for migraine prevention in pregnant women, based on evidence of lower IQ scores in exposed children. It is also contraindicated in women who could become pregnant and are not using effective contraception. In the UK, valproate [must not be used in women and girls of childbearing potential unless a Pregnancy Prevention Programme is in place](https://cqc.org.uk/guidance-providers/adult-social-care/high-risk-medicines-valproate).

**Topiramate.** In 2024, the UK regulator said [topiramate is contraindicated in pregnancy for migraine prevention, and in women who could become pregnant unless a Pregnancy Prevention Programme is followed](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/new-pregnancy-prevention-measures-introduced-for-topiramate). That programme includes effective contraception, a pregnancy test before starting and a signed risk awareness form. [Birth defects rise from about 1 to 3 in 100 pregnancies without the drug to 4 to 9 in 100 with it](https://pharmaceutical-journal.com/article/news/mhra-introduces-new-safety-measures-for-use-of-topiramate-in-pregnancy).

None of this means these drugs are "bad". It means you should tell your doctor if you could become pregnant, are trying to conceive, or are pregnant, before any preventive is chosen. Some women also find that other options on the list are simply a better fit. Our article on [birth control and migraine](/blog/birth-control-and-migraine) covers a related conversation worth having.

## Which one is best for you?

There is no single best preventive: the research suggests several of the common ones work about equally well, so your health and preferences guide the choice. A review summarised by the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Research found that [the main drugs were all more effective than placebo](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/common-migraine-prevention-treatments-found-to-be-equally-effective). Amitriptyline came out as the most effective against placebo, but it [was no more effective than topiramate or propranolol when compared directly](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/common-migraine-prevention-treatments-found-to-be-equally-effective). The review also noted there were fewer head-to-head trials, so the comparison is less certain.

So how do doctors pick? NHS guidance in Scotland says to [base the choice of migraine prophylaxis on age, gender and other health conditions](https://www.rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/tam-treatments-and-medicines-nhs-highland/formularies/highland-formulary/central-nervous-system-formulary/analgesics-formulary-cns/headache-and-migraine-formulary-cns/). In practice, things your doctor may weigh include:

- other health conditions, such as asthma, low or high blood pressure, low mood or sleep problems
- whether you could become pregnant
- other medicines you take
- side effects you would find hardest to live with (tiredness, weight changes, tingling, dizziness)
- what you have already tried

## What can you expect from a preventive?

A preventive is meant to cut your migraine days, not to end migraine for good, and it usually takes a few months to know if it is working. A commonly used definition of success is [at least a 50% reduction in migraine days compared with before treatment](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/migraine-preventive-medications-guidelines-success). That is a real improvement, but it still means some attacks will happen.

On timing, the Migraine Trust says [it can take up to eight weeks to see any effect, and ideally you take a preventive for at least three months to judge it](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/healthcare/treatments/preventive-medicines/). Stopping after two weeks because "nothing happened" is a common reason people give up too early. On the other hand, if side effects bother you, tell your doctor rather than stopping on your own.

It also helps to be realistic about a bumpy start. If your attacks seem the same or slightly worse in the first weeks, that does not automatically mean the treatment has failed, but it is exactly what to bring up at your follow-up.

## What if migraine prevention medication does not help enough?

If the first option does not work or you cannot tolerate it, there are other routes, and trying a second option is common. Many doctors will try a different family before deciding that preventives do not work for you. If several oral options have not helped, the next steps may include [CGRP-targeting treatments](/blog/cgrp-migraine-treatment) or [Botox for chronic migraine](/blog/botox-for-chronic-migraine), which are covered in their own guides.

Non-drug options exist too. The NHS says that if medicines are unsuitable or do not help, [acupuncture may be considered, and NICE states a course of up to 10 sessions over 5 to 8 weeks may be beneficial](https://nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/prevention). You can also read about [how to relieve a migraine without medication](/blog/relieve-migraine-without-medication) and about [magnesium and migraine](/blog/magnesium-and-migraine), where we explain what the science does and does not show.

If you are a parent reading this for your child, the picture is different for young people, so see our guide to [migraine in children and teens](/blog/migraine-in-children-and-teens).

## Questions to ask your doctor about migraine prevention

Going in with a few clear questions makes the appointment more useful, especially because preventives involve trade-offs. Here are some you can take with you:

1. Is a preventive right for me, given how often and how badly my attacks affect me?
2. Why are you suggesting this particular drug over the others?
3. How will it fit with my other health conditions and any other medicines I take?
4. What side effects are most common, and which ones should I report quickly?
5. If I could become pregnant, or want to in the near future, is this drug safe for me, and what safeguards apply?
6. How long should I try it before we judge whether it works?
7. What result would count as a success for me?
8. If it does not help, what is the plan B?
9. How should I take it, and what should I do if I miss a dose or want to stop?

Evidence is much easier to discuss when you can show it. A simple [migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note), a look at your [diary patterns](/blog/migraine-diary-patterns) and a [report for your doctor](/blog/migraine-report-for-doctor) help you both see whether a treatment changed anything. If you are not sure who to book, see [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine).

## Conclusion: a preventive is a conversation, not a quick fix

Migraine prevention medication is an option when attacks are frequent, disabling or hard to treat, and several families of drugs exist: beta blockers, antidepressants, other blood pressure medicines and anti-seizure drugs. They tend to work about as well as each other on average, so the choice depends on your health, your side-effect tolerance and, importantly, whether you could become pregnant. Give a treatment a fair trial of a few months, track your migraine days, and keep your doctor in the loop.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to take migraine prevention medication forever?

Not necessarily. Whether and when to stop is a decision to make with your doctor, who will usually review how well it works and how you tolerate it over time. Do not stop a preventive suddenly on your own, because some of these medicines need to be reduced in a particular way.

### How long does a preventive take to work?

Often a couple of months. The Migraine Trust says it can take up to eight weeks to see an effect and recommends at least three months to judge whether a preventive has helped. Your doctor may plan a follow-up around that point.

### Can I take a preventive and still use my usual migraine pills?

Many people do, because a preventive aims to reduce attacks, not to stop each one. Your doctor can tell you which acute treatment goes with your preventive, so ask about both together. Using acute medicine very often is itself a reason to talk about prevention.

### Are migraine preventives safe if I might get pregnant?

It depends on the drug. Valproate and topiramate carry serious risks in pregnancy, and the Migraine Trust says candesartan is not recommended in pregnancy or when planning one. Tell your doctor about any pregnancy plans before choosing a preventive.
