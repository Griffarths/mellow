Last month, your usual treatment took the edge off within an hour. This time you took it, waited, and two hours later you're still lying in a dark room wondering what went wrong. If you've ever felt that your **migraine medication is not working** the way it used to, you're far from alone.

The good news is that a treatment that works on one attack and barely touches the next usually has an explanation. Timing, nausea, how far the attack has already progressed and how often you take medication all play a part. This article is part of our guide to [migraine treatments](/blog/migraine-treatments), and it explains these factors in plain language, plus what you can write down to make the conversation with your doctor easier. It's general information, not advice on what to take.

**In short**

- Medicines for an attack tend to work best when taken early, [as soon as symptoms begin](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207), so a late start is one of the most common reasons for a weak result.
- Nausea can get in the way: [research on why triptans can fail](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23721285/) shows that migraine slows the stomach and nausea predicts a poorer response to oral triptans.
- One failed attempt isn't proof that a treatment doesn't work: headache specialists at the [American Headache Society](https://americanheadachesociety.org/research/library/what-to-do-when-migraine-treatment-fails) suggest trying a treatment across at least three different attacks.
- Using acute medication too often can backfire: the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/) advises against taking painkillers on more than 2 days a week, because it can lead to more headaches.

## Why does the same migraine treatment work one day and not the next?

Because no two attacks are exactly alike, and several things around the medicine (when you take it, how your stomach is behaving, how advanced the attack already is) change how well it can work. The medicine itself hasn't necessarily changed. What changes is the situation it lands in.

Doctors call medicines taken during an attack "acute" treatment, as opposed to preventive treatment, which is taken regularly to make attacks less frequent. The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/2203_AMF_UnderstandingAcuteTreatment_V4_Digital.pdf) says a sign that an acute medication may not be working as well as it should is when you don't get pain relief or return to normal function within two hours of taking it.

If that happens once, it doesn't mean much. If it keeps happening, it's worth looking at the reasons below, and at your treatment plan with a professional. For a refresher on the main medicine families, see our guide to [migraine medication](/blog/migraine-medication).

## Timing: are you taking it early enough?

Taking your treatment early is the single most repeated piece of advice in migraine care. The Mayo Clinic notes that medicines used to relieve migraine pain [work best when taken as soon as symptoms begin](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207). The NHS gives the same message: if you use painkillers, [take them as soon as the headache starts](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/).

The details differ by medicine. According to the American Migraine Foundation, [triptans appear to be more consistently effective](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/oral-and-intranasal-triptans-for-migraine/) when used earlier in the attack, while symptoms are still mild to moderate. It also says [NSAIDs are typically more useful](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/nsaids-migraine/) when taken early. Clinical trials of sumatriptan, a common triptan, have also found that [pain-free rates at two hours were higher](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-neurological-sciences/article/div-classtitlepain-free-efficacy-of-sumatriptan-in-the-early-treatment-of-migrainediv/E6B237E5D9278B5EA9AF52E8BAFF9BD5) when treatment started while the pain was still mild.

In real life, though, waiting is very human. You're at work, in a lecture, or telling yourself "[maybe it's just a regular headache](/blog/migraine-myths)." You may also hold back because you worry about taking too much medication (more on that below).

A few things can help you act earlier:

- Learn your own early signs. Some people notice yawning, a stiff neck or mood changes hours before the pain, which we describe in our article on [migraine prodrome symptoms](/blog/migraine-prodrome-symptoms).
- Talk to your doctor about when, in your particular pattern, taking treatment makes sense.
- Keep your medicine with you, so "I'll take it when I get home" doesn't become the plan.

## Nausea and vomiting: when the pill can't do its job

Nausea is not only unpleasant, it can change how much of a tablet your body actually absorbs. A review in the journal *Headache* on [why triptan treatment can fail](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23721285/) explains that stomach movement slows down during an attack, which can make the absorption of an oral triptan erratic. It also reports that nausea predicts a poorer response to oral triptans, and that nausea can lead people to delay taking their medicine, which weakens its effect further.

If you vomit, the situation is even trickier, because you can't be sure how much of the tablet stayed in. That's one reason doctors sometimes look beyond standard tablets.

Some options your doctor may discuss with you:

- **Other forms of the same medicine.** The Cleveland Clinic lists that [triptans come as regular and dissolving pills, nasal spray, nasal powder and injection](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/24998-triptans). The American Migraine Foundation says [nasal triptans tend to act faster than tablets](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/oral-and-intranasal-triptans-for-migraine/), and some people find them more effective once the pain has already built up.
- **Anti-sickness medicine.** NHS Scotland's primary care guidance says [anti-sickness medicines can be taken alongside a triptan](https://www.rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/neurology-pathways/headache/acute-treatment-of-migraine-in-primary-care/) and can make oral medicines work better.

Those are things to raise with a doctor, not to try on your own. If nausea is a big part of your attacks, our article on [migraine nausea](/blog/migraine-nausea) explains why it happens and what can ease it.

## When the attack is already well underway

Once a migraine has been going for a while, the brain's pain pathways become more sensitive, and treatment has a harder job. One visible sign of this is allodynia, when normally harmless things hurt: your scalp feels tender, a ponytail is painful, or your glasses suddenly bother you.

This fits what doctors keep saying: early is easier.

If you only realize you're having an attack late (waking up with it, for example), medication may well be less effective, and that isn't a failure on your part. It's one of the reasons it helps to talk with a doctor about a plan for those attacks. Our article on [migraine and sleep](/blog/migraine-and-sleep) looks at why mornings are such a common time for them.

## Triptan not working: why one try isn't the full picture

A triptan that doesn't work once doesn't tell you much, and a triptan that doesn't work for you doesn't mean every triptan will fail. The American Migraine Foundation is clear that [triptans don't work for everyone and won't stop every headache](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/oral-and-intranasal-triptans-for-migraine/), and that if one isn't giving relief, it can make sense to try another.

Here's what the evidence and expert guidance say:

- Headache specialists at the American Headache Society recommend trying a treatment on [at least three different attacks](https://americanheadachesociety.org/research/library/what-to-do-when-migraine-treatment-fails), not just once or repeatedly within the same attack.
- Clinical reference material notes that [lack of response to sumatriptan did not predict lack of response to another triptan](https://gpnotebook.com/en-GB/pages/neurology/management-of-acute-treatment-of-migraine/detailed-information-about-triptans-in-migraine-and-second-line-treatments), so different ones can behave differently in the same person.
- The American Migraine Foundation notes that [combining a triptan with another acute treatment](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/oral-and-intranasal-triptans-for-migraine/) can sometimes work better, and the NHS Scotland guidance says that [a triptan plus an NSAID is more effective than either alone](https://www.rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/neurology-pathways/headache/acute-treatment-of-migraine-in-primary-care/). That's a conversation for your prescriber.

The American Headache Society also reminds readers that the first step when treatment fails is [making sure the diagnosis is correct and complete](https://americanheadachesociety.org/research/library/what-to-do-when-migraine-treatment-fails). Not every headache is a migraine, and not every migraine looks the same. Our article on [triptans](/blog/triptans-explained) covers the types, side effects and safety in more detail.

## Is the pain coming back, or did the medication fail?

These two situations feel similar but are not the same, and your doctor will want to know which one you're describing. Failure means the pain barely eased at all. Recurrence means the medicine helped, then the headache returned hours later or the next day.

Clinical reference material describes [headache recurrence as a recognised issue with triptans, with rates that differ between them](https://gpnotebook.com/en-GB/pages/neurology/management-of-acute-treatment-of-migraine/detailed-information-about-triptans-in-migraine-and-second-line-treatments). That's useful for a doctor to know, because it can influence which option suits your attacks better.

Recurrence can also be part of the natural course of a long attack, and many migraines last for many hours or days. If yours tend to run long, see how long a migraine can last in our article on [how long a migraine lasts](/blog/how-long-does-a-migraine-last), or [why a migraine won't go away](/blog/migraine-that-wont-go-away).

## Can taking medication too often make it work worse?

Yes, in a way: using acute medicine very often can lead to more headaches, which can feel like your treatment has stopped working. This is called medication-overuse headache, and it's well recognized.

The American Migraine Foundation explains that, depending on the medicine, [overuse starts at around ten or more days a month for triptans and 15 or more days for simple painkillers like NSAIDs](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/medication-overuse-headache-3/). The NHS puts it more simply: [try not to take painkillers on more than 2 days a week](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/), as it can lead to more headaches and make migraine harder to treat.

This doesn't mean you should avoid treating attacks out of fear. It means that if you find yourself reaching for medication that often, that's the signal to see your doctor, who may talk to you about preventive options. Our articles on [why you might have a headache every day](/blog/headache-every-day) and [painkillers for headache](/blog/painkiller-for-headache) go further.

## What to write down to talk about it with your doctor

A few weeks of simple notes can turn "it doesn't work" into something your doctor can actually act on. You don't need to write essays, just the same few details each time.

- **When it started** and what you noticed first (early signs, aura, pain).
- **What you took, and when:** which medicine, in which form (tablet, dissolving tablet, spray), and how long after the start.
- **How strong the pain was when you took it:** mild, moderate or severe.
- **Nausea or vomiting,** and whether it started before or after you took it.
- **The result after two hours:** pain gone, partly better, unchanged.
- **Whether it came back,** and when.
- **What was happening around it:** poor sleep, stress, your period, skipped meals.
- **How many days a month you take acute medication.**

## When should you talk to someone without waiting?

Make an appointment if your usual treatment has let you down across several attacks, if you need acute medication often, or if your attacks have changed in a way that worries you. You don't need to wait until you've tried everything.

Our article on [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine) explains who to book and how to prepare. If a headache feels different from your usual ones in a sudden, severe or frightening way, check [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) rather than waiting it out.

## In summary: what often makes the difference

When migraine medication is not working, the cause is often something around the medicine, not the medicine alone. Taking it early, finding a form that suits you when you're nauseous, giving it a fair trial across several attacks and keeping an eye on how often you use it are the points that come up again and again in guidance from the [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207), the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/) and the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/oral-and-intranasal-triptans-for-migraine/).

None of this is a reason to blame yourself. Migraine is unpredictable, and a good treatment plan often takes some adjusting with a professional. A few notes from your next attacks can make that adjustment much easier.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does my migraine medication work sometimes and not others?

Attacks differ in how fast they build, how much nausea they bring and how early you were able to treat them. Each of those changes how much a medicine can do. Your doctor can help you work out which of them applies to you, especially if you bring a few weeks of notes.

### How long should I wait before deciding my migraine medication didn't work?

The American Migraine Foundation says a [lack of pain relief or return to function within two hours](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/2203_AMF_UnderstandingAcuteTreatment_V4_Digital.pdf) is a sign an acute medication may not be working as well as it should. Judging a treatment over several attacks gives a fairer picture than judging it after one. Ask your doctor what to do if the first dose doesn't help.

### If one triptan doesn't work, will the others fail too?

Not necessarily. Clinical reference material notes that [not responding to sumatriptan didn't predict not responding to another triptan](https://gpnotebook.com/en-GB/pages/neurology/management-of-acute-treatment-of-migraine/detailed-information-about-triptans-in-migraine-and-second-line-treatments). Switching or changing the form is a decision for your doctor.

### Can painkillers make migraines worse?

When they're used very often, yes. The NHS warns that taking painkillers [on more than 2 days a week](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/) can cause more headaches. If you're reaching for them that often, talk to your doctor about other options.
