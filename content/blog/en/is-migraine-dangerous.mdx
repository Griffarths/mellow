You're lying in a dark room with a pounding head, and a quiet thought creeps in: "Is migraine dangerous?" Or maybe you saw zigzag lines for twenty minutes this morning and spent the rest of the day wondering if it meant something serious. These worries are very common, and they deserve a calm, honest answer.

So, **is migraine dangerous?** For most people, a migraine attack is painful and disruptive but not harmful in itself. There is, however, a small link between migraine (especially migraine with aura) and stroke, and a few warning signs that should never be ignored. This article explains what studies show, what is still uncertain, and when to get help fast. If you want the bigger picture on how doctors tell migraine apart from other conditions, our [migraine diagnosis guide](/blog/migraine-diagnosis) is a good place to start.

**In short**

- A migraine attack itself is not considered dangerous: the symptoms pass and don't cause lasting disability, even when they feel awful.
- Migraine, especially migraine with aura, is linked to a small increase in the risk of ischaemic stroke, but the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-and-stroke/) stresses that the absolute risk is low.
- Smoking, high blood pressure and combined hormonal contraception matter more than the migraine itself, and they are things you can talk through with a doctor.
- A sudden "worst ever" headache, new speech trouble, weakness on one side or an aura that lasts over an hour needs urgent medical help.

## Is migraine dangerous? The short answer

A migraine attack is usually not dangerous, even though it can feel overwhelming. Migraine is a neurological condition that brings throbbing pain, nausea and sensitivity to light and sound, and the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/) notes that an untreated attack typically lasts between 4 and 72 hours. Then it ends.

What studies do show is a **small increased risk of ischaemic stroke** (a stroke caused by a blocked blood vessel) in people with migraine, according to the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/complications/). Other complications exist but are uncommon, such as medication overuse headache, which the [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20360201) lists among the possible complications. We cover that in our guide to [migraine medication](/blog/migraine-medication).

Two ideas help keep this in perspective. First, "increased risk" is a relative measure, so it tells you how much higher the risk is compared with people without migraine, not how likely it is to happen to you. Second, most of the risk is tied to specific situations, which we'll go through below.

## What do studies say about migraine with aura and stroke?

The link with stroke is clearest in migraine with aura, the form where you get temporary sensory changes such as flashing lights, blind spots or tingling before or during the headache. The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-stroke-reducing-risk/) reports that migraine attacks with aura are associated with stroke and heart disease, and that women might be particularly affected. If you'd like a refresher on what aura is, see [Migraine With Aura: Understanding the Warning Signs](/blog/migraine-with-aura).

Researchers aren't completely sure why. One suggestion is that during aura, blood vessels may narrow temporarily, which could make blood clots more likely in some people. This remains a hypothesis, not a proven chain of cause and effect.

Younger women are the group most often studied. The American Migraine Foundation says that [women under 45 with migraine with aura](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-and-stroke/) have a higher risk of ischaemic stroke than women of the same age without migraine. Even so, it explains that this is a **relative risk**, and that the absolute risk is small: in the US, roughly 2,000 to 3,000 of about 800,000 yearly strokes might be related to migraine. In plain words, strokes are rare in young people, so a higher chance of a rare event is still a low chance.

The same organisation also notes that [aura without a headache](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/silent-migraine/) still counts as migraine with aura when it comes to this risk. If that sounds like you, our article on [silent migraine](/blog/silent-migraine-aura-without-headache) explains how it looks and feels.

## What about migraine without aura?

For migraine without aura, the evidence is less clear and generally more reassuring. Studies don't all agree: one large [Taiwanese population-based cohort study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9339415/) found a higher stroke risk in both people with and without aura, while the link is usually described as stronger with aura.

That mix of results is a good example of what remains uncertain. Differences in study design, age groups and how migraine was diagnosed can all change the numbers. If you mostly have attacks without aura, you can read how this form looks in [Migraine Without Aura: Symptoms, Duration and How It Differs From Migraine With Aura](/blog/migraine-without-aura).

## Which factors matter more than the migraine itself?

The risk grows mainly when migraine with aura is combined with other vascular risk factors. The American Migraine Foundation says the stroke risk goes up when migraine with aura comes with [high blood pressure, obesity and, above all, smoking](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-stroke-reducing-risk/), with smoking described as the most important factor.

### The combined pill

The NHS says that people with migraine with aura are generally advised not to use the [combined contraceptive pill](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/complications/), because it adds to the stroke risk. For migraine without aura, the picture is different, and the decision depends on your other risk factors. The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/birth-control-and-stroke-risk/) has a dedicated page on birth control and stroke risk.

Please don't stop or change any contraception on your own. If you have aura and use hormonal contraception, book a conversation with your doctor or pharmacist, who can look at your whole situation. Our article on [birth control and migraine](/blog/birth-control-and-migraine) explains what to ask.

### What you can influence

The same foundation suggests that people with aura can lower their overall risk by [not smoking, keeping blood pressure under control, eating well, staying active and treating high cholesterol or diabetes](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-stroke-reducing-risk/). None of this is a promise that a stroke will never happen, but it targets the risks that science has actually identified.

## Does migraine damage your brain?

Current evidence does not show that migraine causes lasting brain damage or memory loss. Some brain scans of people with migraine show small spots called white matter lesions, and this alarms many people when they see it in a report.

According to the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-and-brain-lesions/), these lesions are generally not linked to neurological problems and do not appear to raise the risk of cognitive decline. A recent [systematic review of 25 studies](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12721971/) found these lesions are frequent in migraine, particularly with aura, longer disease duration and more frequent attacks, but their clinical meaning remains unclear.

So the honest version is this: the spots exist, they are common, and so far they haven't been shown to harm everyday thinking or function. Researchers are still working out what they mean, and if one shows up on your scan, your doctor is the right person to put it in context.

## Aura or stroke: how can you tell them apart?

The main clue is speed: a stroke starts suddenly, while an aura builds up gradually. The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-stroke-reducing-risk/) explains that each aura symptom usually begins slowly and builds over several minutes, up to 60 minutes. A stroke, in contrast, is almost always a sudden loss of function caused by a blocked or ruptured artery.

A few other differences can help:

- **Type of symptoms:** aura tends to add things (flashing lights, zigzags, tingling), while a stroke tends to take things away (loss of vision, loss of strength, loss of speech).
- **Duration:** aura typically lasts [5 to 60 minutes](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/demystifying-migraine-with-aura/). Very sudden, very brief symptoms or symptoms lasting longer than an hour can be clues that something else is going on.
- **Familiarity:** your usual aura looks the same from one attack to the next. Something brand new deserves attention.

If you see flashing lights and aren't sure whether it's your eyes or your brain, our article on [flashing lights in the eyes](/blog/flashing-lights-in-eyes-migraine-aura) walks through the options.

This is general information, not a way to diagnose yourself. When in doubt, treat symptoms as urgent.

## Which signs mean you should get help quickly?

Sudden, severe or unusual symptoms always justify urgent care, even if you have a history of migraine. Call your local emergency number (999 in the UK, 911 in the US) or go to the emergency department if you have any of the following.

- **A sudden, extremely painful headache.** The [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/17876-thunderclap-headaches) describes a thunderclap headache as one that peaks in under a minute, and advises going to the ER if it happens for the first time, because some causes can be fatal without prompt treatment.
- **New trouble speaking or understanding.** The NHS lists [problems speaking or remembering things, loss of vision, drowsiness or confusion, and seizures](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/) among reasons to call 999.
- **Weakness or numbness on one side.** The NHS says to call 999 if you [cannot move or have weakness in an arm, leg or one side of the face](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/stroke/symptoms/). Any single stroke symptom is an emergency, even if it fades.
- **Headache with fever, stiff neck, confusion or double vision.** The [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20360201) lists these as reasons to seek emergency care.
- **A new kind of pain.** The [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/should-you-ever-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) includes a level of pain you've never felt before and vision changes or speech trouble you haven't had before.

For aura that drags on, the NHS says to get an [urgent GP appointment or call NHS 111](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/) if it lasts longer than an hour. Our guide on [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) gives more examples of when to go and when you can wait.

## When can you see a doctor without any rush?

Some situations don't call for the ER but are still worth a proper appointment. These include attacks that are getting more frequent, a pattern that has changed, headaches that never really leave you, or a headache that doesn't respond to what usually helps. The American Migraine Foundation also recommends that people with aura get [regular evaluations](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-stroke-reducing-risk/).

It is also worth booking a visit if you have aura and use hormonal contraception, smoke, or have been told your blood pressure is high. If you've never had your headaches properly assessed, you might also like [When to See a Doctor for Migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine), which explains who to book and how to prepare. And if you're wondering whether your pain is migraine or something else, [Migraine vs Headache](/blog/migraine-vs-headache) and [Why Do I Have a Headache Every Day?](/blog/headache-every-day) can help you sort it out.

## In practice: staying reassured without ignoring the signs

The most useful stance is calm awareness: take the small stroke link seriously without letting it frighten you. For most people living with migraine, attacks are painful and tiring but not dangerous, and the biggest lever you have is looking after the risks you can influence.

A few practical habits:

1. **Know your usual attack.** Notice what your aura looks like and how long it lasts, so you can spot when something is different.
2. **Keep an eye on the basics.** Not smoking, checking blood pressure and staying active are good for your head and your heart. Our [migraine diary guide](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) shows what is worth writing down.
3. **Tell every doctor you have aura.** It matters for decisions about contraception and other treatments.
4. **Act on sudden or new symptoms.** If something feels wrong, get checked. You will not be wasting anyone's time.

If you'd like to understand why attacks happen in the first place, read [What Causes Migraines? What Happens in Your Brain](/blog/what-causes-migraines). And for the wider numbers, [Migraine by the Numbers](/blog/migraine-by-the-numbers) puts migraine in global context.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a migraine kill you?

A migraine attack on its own is not considered life-threatening. The concern is a small increased stroke risk in some people, mainly those with aura, as described by the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/complications/). Sudden, unusual symptoms should still be checked urgently, because they may not be a migraine.

### Does having migraine with aura mean I will have a stroke?

No. The American Migraine Foundation explains that the higher risk is relative and the [absolute risk is small](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-and-stroke/), especially in young women. Not smoking and keeping blood pressure healthy are the most useful steps.

### Can migraine cause brain damage?

Current evidence doesn't show that it does. The American Migraine Foundation says the [white matter spots sometimes seen on scans](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-and-brain-lesions/) are generally not linked to neurological problems or cognitive decline, although researchers are still studying what they mean.

### How long can an aura last before I should worry?

Aura typically lasts 5 to 60 minutes. The NHS advises seeking an [urgent GP appointment or calling NHS 111](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/) if it lasts longer than an hour, and calling 999 if you have speech trouble, weakness or loss of vision.

### Should I go to the ER if my migraine feels different from usual?

If the change is sudden, severe or comes with confusion, speech trouble, weakness, fever or a stiff neck, yes. The [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/should-you-ever-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) lists these among the red flags. For milder changes in your pattern, book a routine appointment.
