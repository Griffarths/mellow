You stand up from the sofa and the room tilts. Or you walk through a busy supermarket and the floor feels like a boat deck, with no headache in sight. A few hours later you are exhausted, a bit nauseous, and wondering what just happened. It could be **vestibular migraine**, a form of migraine that shows up as dizziness rather than head pain.

For some people, the answer is **vestibular migraine**: a form of migraine where the main symptom is dizziness, vertigo or a feeling of being unsteady, rather than head pain. It is one of the many forms described on our [migraine symptoms](/blog/migraine-symptoms) page. This article explains what vestibular migraine looks like, how long it lasts, how it differs from other causes of dizziness, and when to get it checked.

**In short**

- Vestibular migraine is a type of migraine that affects the balance system, so it can cause vertigo, dizziness or unsteadiness, and [it does not always involve head pain](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/25217-vestibular-migraine).
- Episodes typically last [between 5 minutes and 72 hours](https://ichd-3.org/appendix/a1-migraine/a1-6-episodic-syndromes-that-may-be-associated-with-migraine/a1-6-6-vestibular-migraine/), and the diagnosis rests on your history because [there is no single test for it](https://migrainetrust.org/understand-migraine/types-of-migraine/vestibular/).
- Other causes of vertigo, such as inner ear problems, need to be ruled out by a healthcare professional, so recurring dizziness is always worth mentioning.
- Sudden severe dizziness together with weakness, speech trouble, double vision or a sudden severe headache needs [emergency care](https://www.mayoclinic.org/symptoms/dizziness/basics/when-to-see-doctor/sym-20050886).

## What is vestibular migraine?

Vestibular migraine is a type of migraine in which the main symptoms involve the vestibular system, the part of your inner ear and brain that controls balance and your sense of where your body is in space. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/25217-vestibular-migraine) describes it as a migraine where dizziness and vertigo take center stage, and where head pain may or may not be there.

Doctors still do not fully know why migraine affects balance. The general idea, as with other migraine attacks, is that the brain becomes temporarily more sensitive and processes signals differently. If you want the bigger picture, our article on [what causes migraines](/blog/what-causes-migraines) walks through what happens in the brain.

You may also see older names such as "migrainous vertigo" or "migraine-associated vertigo". If you come across unfamiliar words at the doctor's office, the [migraine glossary](/blog/migraine-glossary) explains them in plain language.

## What does a vestibular migraine feel like?

Vestibular migraine can feel like spinning, rocking, floating or simply being off balance, often with nausea and sensitivity to light or sound. The experience varies a lot from one person to another, and even from one attack to the next.

According to [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/25217-vestibular-migraine), symptoms can include:

- Dizziness or vertigo (the feeling that you or the room is moving)
- Balance problems
- Sensitivity to light and noise
- Nausea
- Blurred vision or flashing lights
- Difficulty focusing
- Sweating or flushing
- Strong tiredness afterwards

The Migraine Trust also notes that [vertigo, imbalance, nausea and vomiting](https://migrainetrust.org/understand-migraine/types-of-migraine/vestibular/) are common, and that the vertigo does not always happen at the same time as a headache. That timing mismatch is one reason the condition can be hard to spot.

Some people feel worse with movement, like turning the head quickly or being in a car. Others struggle with busy visual scenes: scrolling, supermarket aisles, patterns or crowds. If you also get zigzag lines or blind spots, our guide to [migraine with aura](/blog/migraine-with-aura) may help you tell those symptoms apart.

## Can you have vestibular migraine without a headache?

Yes, you can. Vestibular migraine does not always come with a headache, and some people only ever notice the dizziness.

The [Migraine Trust](https://migrainetrust.org/understand-migraine/types-of-migraine/vestibular/) states that many people with vestibular migraine will not always have headaches. That does not mean the dizziness is "just in your head" or unrelated to migraine. It means migraine can show up through different doors.

This is similar to what happens with [silent migraine](/blog/silent-migraine-aura-without-headache), where aura appears without head pain. A key clue for doctors is that you usually have a history of migraine at some point in your life, even if the headaches have faded or are mild.

The catch: when there is no headache, nobody thinks of migraine first. That is why people sometimes go years without a clear answer, and why keeping notes can help (more on that below).

## How long does a vestibular migraine episode last?

An episode can last anywhere from a few minutes to about three days. The international headache classification (ICHD-3) describes vestibular symptoms [lasting between 5 minutes and 72 hours](https://ichd-3.org/appendix/a1-migraine/a1-6-episodic-syndromes-that-may-be-associated-with-migraine/a1-6-6-vestibular-migraine/).

Some people have short bursts of vertigo, others feel unsteady for most of a day. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/25217-vestibular-migraine) adds that tiredness or mild dizziness can linger for up to three days after an episode.

For comparison with typical attacks, see our article on [how long a migraine lasts](/blog/how-long-does-a-migraine-last).

## How is it different from other causes of dizziness?

The main differences are timing, triggers and accompanying symptoms, but only a professional can sort out which cause fits you. Many conditions can make you dizzy, and some of the most common ones involve the inner ear. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vertigo/) lists BPPV, labyrinthitis, vestibular neuritis and Ménière's disease among the usual causes of vertigo, and also mentions that migraine can be a cause.

Here is a rough, informational guide to how they tend to differ:

- **BPPV** (benign paroxysmal positional vertigo): [brief spinning triggered by head position changes](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/bppv/symptoms-causes/syc-20370055), such as rolling over in bed or looking up. The Mayo Clinic says symptoms most often last less than a minute.
- **Ménière's disease**: vertigo episodes that [usually last from 20 minutes to several hours](https://www.mayoclinic.org/health/menieres-disease/DS00535), often with ringing in the ear, a feeling of ear fullness and hearing loss that comes and goes.
- **Vestibular migraine**: episodes from minutes to days, often with light or sound sensitivity, a past or present history of migraine, and sometimes a headache or visual symptoms.

Be careful with this list. Real life is messier: hearing symptoms and migraine features can overlap, and it is possible to have more than one condition. That is exactly why a proper assessment matters, rather than guessing from a table.

## How is vestibular migraine diagnosed?

Doctors diagnose vestibular migraine mainly from your story, after checking that nothing else explains your symptoms. The [Migraine Trust](https://migrainetrust.org/understand-migraine/types-of-migraine/vestibular/) explains that the diagnosis relies on your history and symptoms, because there is no reliable test that confirms it.

The ICHD-3 criteria for vestibular migraine include:

- At least five episodes of vestibular symptoms of [moderate or severe intensity](https://ichd-3.org/appendix/a1-migraine/a1-6-episodic-syndromes-that-may-be-associated-with-migraine/a1-6-6-vestibular-migraine/), lasting 5 minutes to 72 hours
- A current or past history of migraine, with or without aura
- Migraine features (such as a [one-sided throbbing headache](/blog/migraine-behind-the-eye), light and sound sensitivity, or visual aura) during at least half of the episodes
- No better explanation from another diagnosis or vestibular disorder

In practice, you might see a GP or primary care doctor first, and then an ENT (ear, nose and throat) specialist or a neurologist. Hearing tests or scans are sometimes used to rule out other causes, not to prove migraine. ## What can you do to keep track and limit episodes?

A symptom diary is the most useful thing you can bring to an appointment, and some everyday habits may help reduce how often episodes happen. None of this replaces medical advice, and treatment choices belong to you and your doctor.

### Note what happens around each episode

Write down the date, how long the dizziness lasted, what it felt like (spinning, rocking, unsteady), and whether you had a headache, nausea, or sensitivity to light or sound. Add what happened in the hours before: poor sleep, a skipped meal, stress, your period, a weather change. Our guide to the [migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) shows what is worth writing down.

That information helps a doctor see whether the episodes fit a migraine pattern. It also fits with [identifying your migraine triggers](/blog/identify-migraine-triggers) more generally.

### Look at common triggers

[Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/25217-vestibular-migraine) lists triggers such as certain foods (for example aged cheese or deli meats), stress, fatigue and lack of sleep, hormonal changes (periods or menopause) and weather changes. Triggers differ a lot between people, and food triggers in particular are less clear-cut than people think, so a diary is a better guide than a long list of bans.

### Ask about treatment options

Cleveland Clinic notes that there is [no cure, but there are ways to manage it](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/25217-vestibular-migraine): medicines can help prevent episodes or ease symptoms, and more rest, regular activity and stress management can help too. The Migraine Trust says that [preventive migraine treatments](https://migrainetrust.org/understand-migraine/types-of-migraine/vestibular/) are often used for vestibular migraine. Which option suits you, if any, is a decision to make with your doctor. Nausea can be a big part of the picture, and our article on [migraine nausea](/blog/migraine-nausea) covers that side.

## When should you get help, and when is it an emergency?

Get help soon for dizziness that keeps coming back, and call emergency services for sudden severe dizziness with other warning signs. The Mayo Clinic advises seeing a healthcare professional for [dizziness or vertigo that keeps returning, starts suddenly, disrupts daily life or has no clear cause](https://www.mayoclinic.org/symptoms/dizziness/basics/when-to-see-doctor/sym-20050886).

Seek emergency care if new, severe dizziness comes with any of these, according to the [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/symptoms/dizziness/basics/when-to-see-doctor/sym-20050886):

- A sudden severe headache or chest pain
- Loss of feeling or movement in an arm or leg
- Trouble walking or stumbling
- Facial weakness
- Double vision or sudden hearing changes
- Confusion or slurred speech
- Fainting, seizures or ongoing vomiting

In the UK, the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vertigo/) says to call 999 or go to A&E if vertigo comes with double vision or loss of vision, hearing loss, trouble speaking, or weakness, numbness or tingling in the arms or legs. Dizziness on its own is, according to the Mayo Clinic, [generally unlikely to be a sign of stroke](https://www.mayoclinic.org/symptoms/dizziness/basics/when-to-see-doctor/sym-20050886), but if you are unsure, it is always fine to ask for urgent advice. Our guide on [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) gives more detail.

## What to remember

If your migraines come with spinning, rocking or unsteadiness, or you get dizzy episodes with no headache at all, vestibular migraine is one possibility worth discussing. It is a recognized form of migraine, and you are not imagining things.

Because other conditions can look similar, the most helpful steps are simple: keep a short diary, notice triggers and timing, and book an appointment to have it properly assessed. Take the warning signs above seriously, and otherwise be reassured that this is a well-known pattern that doctors can work with. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can migraine cause dizziness without a headache?

Yes. Vestibular migraine can cause vertigo or unsteadiness with little or no head pain, and the [Migraine Trust](https://migrainetrust.org/understand-migraine/types-of-migraine/vestibular/) notes that many people do not always have headaches. A history of migraine, even in the past, is an important clue for doctors.

### Is vestibular migraine dangerous?

Vestibular migraine itself is a migraine variant, not a sign of a dangerous disease. However, other causes of dizziness sometimes need urgent care, so [new severe dizziness with weakness, speech trouble, double vision or a sudden severe headache](https://www.mayoclinic.org/symptoms/dizziness/basics/when-to-see-doctor/sym-20050886) should be checked right away.

### Is there a test for vestibular migraine?

No single test confirms it. The [Migraine Trust](https://migrainetrust.org/understand-migraine/types-of-migraine/vestibular/) explains that the diagnosis depends on your history and symptoms, and tests are mostly used to rule out other conditions.

### Can hormones or my period trigger vestibular migraine?

They can for some people. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/25217-vestibular-migraine) lists hormonal changes, such as those around periods or menopause, among possible triggers. Tracking your episodes alongside your cycle can show whether you notice a link, and our article on [migraine and periods](/blog/migraine-and-periods) goes deeper.
