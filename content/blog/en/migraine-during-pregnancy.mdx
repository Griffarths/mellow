You find out you're pregnant and your first thought is about the baby. Your second might be about your head: will migraine during pregnancy get worse, disappear, or turn into something you don't recognise? Or maybe you're not pregnant yet, but you're thinking about it and wondering what to expect.

Migraine during pregnancy changes for many women, and more often for the better than the worse. Hormones are one of the big players, and they are one of the many [migraine triggers](/blog/migraine-triggers) worth understanding. This article walks you through what usually happens, what the studies say (including the less comfortable findings), and when it's worth calling a professional.

**In short**

- Many women with migraine have fewer attacks during pregnancy: the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/2202_AMF_MigraineInPregnancy_V5_Digital.pdf) reports that 50 to 80 percent see a reduction, though every pregnancy is different.
- The first trimester can be rougher, and migraine with aura is less likely to calm down.
- Migraine is linked to a higher risk of some pregnancy complications, such as preeclampsia, but most women with migraine have healthy pregnancies, and the link does not mean migraine causes them.
- Migraine often returns after birth, sometimes within days, and breastfeeding may delay it.

## What happens to migraine during pregnancy?

For most women, migraine gets better as pregnancy goes on, but this is a tendency, not a promise. The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/2202_AMF_MigraineInPregnancy_V5_Digital.pdf) notes that 50 to 80 percent of pregnant women with migraine have fewer attacks, and that frequency and severity can even differ from one pregnancy to the next in the same person.

The [Migraine Trust](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/migraine-in-pregnancy/) says migraine without aura improves after the first three months for about 7 out of 10 women. That also means roughly 3 in 10 don't see that improvement, and a small group get worse.

Some women also get migraine for the first time while pregnant. The same organisation reports that around 16 percent of women have their first migraines during pregnancy, most often in the first trimester. If that's you, it doesn't mean something is wrong, but it's a good reason to mention it to your midwife or doctor (more on that below).

## Why do hormones change the picture?

Estrogen (the main female sex hormone) is the most likely reason, because it rises steadily during pregnancy instead of going up and down like it does across your cycle. The Migraine Trust explains that [stable oestrogen levels](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/migraine-in-pregnancy/) are the likely reason migraine improves after the first trimester.

This fits what many women already notice with their periods. A fall in estrogen is a well-known trigger, which is why attacks cluster around menstruation (we explain this in [Migraine and Periods](/blog/migraine-and-periods)). During pregnancy, the hormone doesn't drop in that way for months, so one trigger is quietly switched off.

It's worth being honest about the limits here: researchers think hormones are central, but migraine is not caused by estrogen alone. Sleep, stress, hydration and genetics still play a role, which is why not everyone improves.

### Why can the first trimester feel worse?

The first trimester is often harder because hormones are still shifting and early pregnancy symptoms pile up. The [Migraine Trust](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/migraine-in-pregnancy/) points out that morning sickness can mean eating and drinking less, which can lead to low blood sugar and dehydration, both of which may set off an attack.

Tiredness, disturbed sleep and the stress of big news can add to the mix. If you want to see whether sleep plays a part for you, [Migraine and Sleep](/blog/migraine-and-sleep) goes into the link in detail.

## What if you have migraine with aura?

Migraine with aura (the visual or sensory changes that come before or during an attack) is less likely to improve during pregnancy. The Migraine Trust states that if you have migraine with aura, you are [more likely to keep having attacks](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/migraine-in-pregnancy/) during pregnancy.

A review in [Practical Neurology](https://practicalneurology.com/diseases-diagnoses/headache-pain/migraine-during-pregnancy/31515/) adds that among the small number of women (4 to 8 percent) whose migraines get worse in pregnancy, migraine with aura appears over-represented. Small numbers, but useful to know so you aren't surprised.

If you're not sure whether what you get counts as aura, our guide to [migraine with aura](/blog/migraine-with-aura) explains the warning signs. Worth knowing too: auras can show up without much pain at all (see [Silent Migraine](/blog/silent-migraine-aura-without-headache)). During pregnancy, any new or unusual visual symptom is something to mention to your care team rather than assume it's "just your aura".

## Migraine and pregnancy complications: what do studies say?

Studies show a link between migraine and certain pregnancy complications, but a link is not a cause, and the absolute risks stay fairly small. The biggest piece of evidence comes from a [study presented by the American Academy of Neurology](https://www.aan.com/PressRoom/Home/PressRelease/4959). Researchers at Brigham and Women's Hospital followed more than 30,000 pregnancies in about 19,000 women over 20 years, using data from the Nurses' Health Study 2.

Here is what they found:

- Women with migraine had a **17 percent higher risk of preterm delivery**, a **28 percent higher risk of gestational high blood pressure** and a **40 percent higher risk of preeclampsia** than women without migraine.
- Preeclampsia (a pregnancy condition involving high blood pressure) affected 6 percent of pregnancies in women with migraine, compared with 3 percent in women without.
- Migraine was not linked to gestational diabetes or low birthweight.

What does that mean for you? The study doesn't show that migraine causes these problems, and it can't tell any one woman what will happen to her. It mostly suggests that if you have migraine, your care team may want to keep a slightly closer eye on your blood pressure. This is one more reason to tell them about your migraine history early.

The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/2202_AMF_MigraineInPregnancy_V5_Digital.pdf) also notes that migraine symptoms that first appear or get worse during pregnancy can be a sign of a higher risk of complications, which is another good reason to speak up.

## Which signs mean you should get help quickly?

A severe headache or vision changes during pregnancy should be checked quickly, even if you're used to migraine. The NHS lists a [severe headache or problems with vision](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/common-symptoms/pregnancy-symptoms-you-need-to-get-help-for/), such as blurring or seeing flashing lights, among the signs of pre-eclampsia that need urgent medical advice. In the UK that means calling your midwife, GP or NHS 111. In the US, call your OB-GYN or midwife right away, and go to emergency care if you can't reach anyone.

Why the caution? Because pre-eclampsia can look a bit like a migraine at first, and flashing lights are also a classic aura symptom. You can't tell the two apart on your own, and that's fine: it isn't your job to work out which one it is.

[Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/why-do-women-experience-pregnancy-headaches/) advises talking to your ob-gyn about headaches in pregnancy, partly because treatments you used before may not be right now. Get in touch promptly if:

- the headache is new, unusually severe, or different from your usual migraine
- it doesn't go away or comes back with other symptoms such as vision changes or swelling of the face, hands or feet
- you have pain below the ribs, or you're vomiting a lot
- you're past 20 weeks and a new headache appears

This isn't meant to scare you. Most headaches in pregnancy are harmless, but pregnancy is one of those moments where "better to check" is the right call. To learn more about urgent situations in general, read [When to Go to the ER for a Migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine).

## What about medicines during pregnancy?

Which medicines are appropriate during pregnancy depends on your situation and your stage of pregnancy, so this is a conversation for your doctor, not for an article. What we can say in general is that some migraine medicines are handled differently in pregnancy, and the guidance can change from one trimester to the next.

That's why the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/2202_AMF_MigraineInPregnancy_V5_Digital.pdf) stresses planning ahead: if you're thinking about pregnancy, talk to your provider about your current medicines before you conceive. If you're already pregnant, don't start, stop or swap anything on your own.

If you want to understand the main families of treatments before that appointment, you can read [Migraine Medication Explained](/blog/migraine-medication) and [Migraine Prevention Medication](/blog/migraine-prevention-medication). They're informative only and don't replace advice for your own pregnancy.

One more thing: repeated painkiller use can itself cause headaches. [Medication overuse headache](/blog/medication-overuse-headache) is worth reading about if you find you're reaching for something often.

## Will migraines come back after birth?

For many women they do, and sometimes quickly, because estrogen falls sharply after delivery. The Migraine Trust notes that some mothers have a [bad attack within a couple of days](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/migraine-in-pregnancy/) of giving birth, which may be due to that sudden drop.

One prospective study published in [Internal Medicine](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/internalmedicine/51/22/51_51.8542/_article) tracked recurrence after delivery. It found that 63 percent of women had migraine again at 1 month, 75 percent at 3 months, 78 percent at 6 months and 87.5 percent at 12 months. At 1 month, recurrence was 50 percent in women who breastfed and 86.4 percent in those who didn't, and breastfeeding was linked to lower recurrence up to six months.

Breastfeeding seems to help because estrogen stays lower and steadier, but it's not a guarantee, and a study like this can't prove cause and effect. Newborn life adds its own triggers: broken sleep, skipped meals, stress. You may also feel foggy and drained, as described in [Migraine Brain Fog](/blog/migraine-brain-fog).

A severe headache after birth also deserves a call. The NHS list of [pregnancy symptoms that need help](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/common-symptoms/pregnancy-symptoms-you-need-to-get-help-for/) is a useful reference, and your midwife or doctor can tell you what's normal for the weeks after delivery.

## How to prepare and talk to your doctor

The most useful thing you can bring to an appointment is a clear record of your migraines before and during pregnancy. A few weeks of notes (dates, duration, intensity, aura or not, and suspected triggers) help your doctor see whether things are improving, staying the same or changing in a way that needs attention.

Here is what to note and mention:

- **How often and how long** your attacks last, and whether that has changed since you became pregnant
- **Whether you get aura**, and whether it has changed or is new
- **Any new symptoms**: vision changes, swelling, pain under the ribs, a headache that is different from your usual ones
- **What you use now** for attacks and prevention, so your doctor can review it
- **Your cycle and hormonal history**, including past hormonal contraception (see [Birth Control and Migraine](/blog/birth-control-and-migraine))

Our guide to [what to write in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) shows how to keep this simple, and [Migraine Report for Doctor](/blog/migraine-report-for-doctor) explains how to turn it into a short summary. If you're not sure who to book, [When to See a Doctor for Migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine) can help.

## To sum up

Migraine during pregnancy often gets better after the first trimester, especially migraine without aura, but it can stay the same or get worse, and it often returns after birth. Studies link migraine to a slightly higher risk of complications like preeclampsia, which is mainly a reason for good follow-up, not for fear.

Keep your notes, tell your midwife or doctor about your migraine history early, and get quick advice for any severe or unusual headache or vision change. You're not expected to figure it out alone.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do migraines get worse in early pregnancy?

They can for some women. The Migraine Trust says that in the first three months, morning sickness, low blood sugar and dehydration can make migraine worse, and that most women who improve do so after that point. If your attacks are new or more frequent, tell your doctor or midwife.

### Is migraine during pregnancy dangerous for the baby?

Migraine itself is not thought to harm the baby, but studies link it to a higher risk of preterm delivery, gestational high blood pressure and preeclampsia, according to the American Academy of Neurology. These are risks to monitor, not certainties, which is why your care team may check your blood pressure closely.

### Can migraine start during pregnancy?

Yes. The Migraine Trust reports that around 16 percent of women have their first migraines while pregnant, most often in the first trimester. A new or severe headache in pregnancy should still be checked, because other causes need to be ruled out.

### Does breastfeeding help with migraine?

It may. In a prospective study in Internal Medicine, 50 percent of breastfeeding women had migraine again at one month after delivery, compared with 86.4 percent of those who did not breastfeed. This doesn't mean everyone will be protected, and it isn't a reason to feel pressure about feeding choices.
