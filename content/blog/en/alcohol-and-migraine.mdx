You have one glass of wine with friends and wake up the next morning with a pounding head. Or you drink nothing for weeks, then a single cocktail seems to bring on a full attack. So you start to wonder about alcohol and migraine: is it the alcohol, the red wine, or something else about that evening?

The link between **alcohol and migraine** is real for some people, but it is much less simple than "alcohol causes migraines". Studies show a big gap between what people believe and what they can reliably reproduce. This article explains what is actually studied (red wine, dehydration, disrupted sleep), which ideas deserve a little more nuance, and how to test your own reaction calmly. Alcohol is one trigger among many, so it also helps to see it in the bigger picture of your [migraine triggers](/blog/migraine-triggers).

**In short**

- Around one in three people with migraine say alcohol is a trigger, but in careful studies it only provokes an attack consistently in a minority.
- Red wine is the drink people blame most often, yet research does not show that it is worse than other alcohol for everyone.
- Dehydration, a night of broken sleep and stress around social events may explain part of what feels like an "alcohol attack".
- You can test your own reaction with a simple diary, tracking the drink, the amount, your sleep and your stress, rather than avoiding everything by default.

## Is alcohol a migraine trigger for everyone?

No: alcohol is a trigger for some people with migraine, not for all of them. In [a review of the research](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10194-008-0006-1), about one third of people with migraine reported alcohol as a trigger at least occasionally, but only around 10% reported it as a frequent trigger in retrospective studies.

A larger survey points the same way. [In a study of 2,197 people with migraine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30565341/), 35.6% said alcohol was a trigger. Yet red wine, the drink most often named, consistently led to an attack in only 8.8% of participants. The authors concluded that a drink on its own is probably not enough, and that its effect may depend on a fluctuating "trigger threshold" (how much your brain can tolerate on a given day).

That idea fits what many people notice: the same glass of wine is fine one evening and a problem another time. Poor sleep, stress, hormones and skipped meals can all lower your threshold. If you want to understand why, our guide on [what causes migraines](/blog/what-causes-migraines) explains how the migraine brain reacts to changes.

## Is red wine really the culprit?

Red wine is the most blamed drink, but science has not proven that it is the worst one for everyone. It is the most commonly reported trigger among alcoholic drinks, yet [the American Migraine Foundation notes](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/top-10-migraine-triggers/) that studies show other types of alcohol are just as likely, and sometimes more often, the culprit.

There is one famous experiment in favour of red wine. [In a small study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2894493/?dopt=Abstract) of people who believed red wine triggered their migraines, 9 of 11 had a typical attack after drinking red wine in disguise, while none of the 8 who were given vodka did. The authors suggested red wine contains something that provokes migraine that is neither alcohol nor tyramine. This was a small, older study, though, and it only included people who were already convinced they reacted to red wine.

More recent evidence is less clear. [A 2025 systematic review of cross-sectional studies](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11826089/) found no conclusive evidence that wine consumption increases or decreases the risk of migraine, and the included studies had a moderate risk of bias. [A review published in 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12655429/) similarly notes that red wine is cited very often as a trigger, a finding not always confirmed by the few prospective studies (studies that follow people over time and record their drinks as they happen).

So what does this mean for you? Red wine may be a real trigger for you personally, but it is not a guaranteed one, and swapping it for white wine, beer or spirits will not automatically protect you.

## Why can one drink set off an attack?

Researchers do not have one single answer, and probably several things add up. Here is what is described in the literature.

### Alcohol itself can cause a headache

Alcohol can cause its own kind of headache, separate from migraine. [A 2025 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12655429/) describes alcohol-induced headache as a throbbing headache on both sides of the head that gets worse with physical activity. In people with migraine, an attack can start within a few hours of drinking. In the large survey above, [the attack came within three hours in about one third of people, and within 10 hours in 90%](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30565341/).

### Dehydration

Alcohol makes you pass more urine, which can leave you dehydrated. [The NHS explains](https://111.wales.nhs.uk/hangover/) that dehydration causes many of the symptoms of a hangover. [Mayo Clinic lists](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/hangovers/symptoms-causes/syc-20373012) headache, tiredness and dizziness among them, plus sensitivity to light and sound, which will sound familiar if you live with migraine.

This is also why a hangover headache and a migraine can be hard to tell apart the next morning. If the pain is mostly one-sided, throbbing, and comes with nausea, you may be dealing with a migraine that a bad night helped to start. Our article on [migraine nausea](/blog/migraine-nausea) can help if that is part of your attacks.

### Inflammation and other compounds

Mayo Clinic also says alcohol [triggers an inflammatory response from the immune system](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/hangovers/symptoms-causes/syc-20373012) and can make blood sugar drop. Whether this matters specifically for migraine is not settled, and researchers are still studying which components of drinks are involved. Be cautious about any article that names one "guilty" ingredient with certainty.

## Broken sleep: the quiet accomplice

Poor sleep after drinking is one of the most likely reasons a night out ends in a migraine. Alcohol may help you fall asleep faster, but it tends to disturb the second half of the night. [According to the US National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6707127/), alcohol can lead to frequent awakenings and fragmented sleep later in the night, and even a moderate dose taken up to 6 hours before bedtime can increase wakefulness in the second half of sleep.

Sleep and migraine are tightly linked, in both directions. You may also go to bed later than usual, skip your normal routine or sleep in the next day, all of which can matter. If you often wake up with a headache, our article on [migraine and sleep](/blog/migraine-and-sleep) goes deeper into why.

Social evenings often come with other triggers too: a late dinner, skipped meals, noise, bright lights, stress or excitement. This is partly why [weekend migraines](/blog/weekend-migraines) are so common, since the weekend is when routines (and drinks) change.

## Alcohol and migraine: the ideas worth nuancing

Some beliefs are widespread, but the evidence is more mixed. Here are the most common ones.

**"Red wine is always the worst."** As covered above, red wine is the most reported trigger, but it is [not always confirmed](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12655429/) in prospective studies, and other drinks can be just as likely to be blamed.

**"If I get a migraine after drinking, alcohol caused it."** Not necessarily. [The American Migraine Foundation describes an Italian study](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/top-10-migraine-triggers/) where 307 people with migraine without aura filled in a questionnaire each time they drank. The researchers found no link between alcohol and attacks, but did find a link between stressful events and attacks. This suggests people who drink when stressed may mistake alcohol for the trigger.

**"People with migraine who drink always have more attacks."** Large analyses do not show this. [A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of 22 studies](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s10194-023-01653-7) found the risk of migraine was lower in people who drank alcohol than in non-drinkers. This does not mean alcohol protects you. It probably reflects that many people with migraine drink less or stop altogether because they notice (or fear) a link, which makes the comparison tricky.

**"A prospective study proves alcohol is harmless."** Not that either. [A 2022 prospective study](https://headachejournal.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/head.14428) in English-speaking countries found no significant increase in the chance of an attack in the 24 hours after drinking, on average. But averages hide individuals: some people react strongly, others not at all.

The honest summary is that alcohol can be a trigger for some people, it is rarely the only one, and the picture varies a lot from person to person.

## How to test your own reaction to alcohol

The most useful approach is to observe patterns over time, not to guess from one bad evening. A simple diary can help you see whether alcohol, a specific drink, or something around it is behind your attacks. For the full method, see [how to identify your migraine triggers](/blog/identify-migraine-triggers), and for what to write down, our [migraine diary guide](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note).

Here is a gentle way to do it:

1. **Note every drink, not only the bad ones.** Write down the type (red wine, white wine, beer, spirits), roughly how much, and what time you drank. The point is to also record the evenings when nothing happened.
2. **Note the context.** Sleep the night before and after, stress level, meals, hydration, and where you are in your cycle if relevant. Hormones can shift your sensitivity, as explained in our article on [menstrual migraine](/blog/menstrual-migraine).
3. **Record when the pain starts.** A headache within a few hours is not the same as one that arrives the next morning after a short night.
4. **Look for repeats.** After several occasions, check whether one drink, one amount or one situation keeps coming back before attacks. One evening proves nothing.
5. **Change one thing at a time.** If you want to know about red wine, compare it with the same context (same meal, same sleep routine), rather than switching drink, time and company all at once.

This is an observation method, not a test to push yourself through. You are never obliged to provoke an attack to prove a point. If a drink has clearly set off severe attacks before, you can simply choose to skip it. Also keep in mind that a trigger rarely works alone: you may tolerate a glass of wine on a calm, well-rested day and not on a stressful one. Our article on [migraine and food](/blog/migraine-and-food) covers how other foods fit into the same picture.

## What about alcohol and general health?

Beyond migraine, there is no completely risk-free level of alcohol. [The NHS explains](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/alcohol-advice/the-risks-of-drinking-too-much/) that UK guidance calls drinking under 14 units a week "low risk" rather than "safe", because no level of drinking is entirely without risk. [The 2025 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12655429/) on migraine and alcohol also states that there is no safe dose of alcohol, and therefore that avoidance should be recommended.

That does not mean you must cut it out because of your migraine. It means that your choice is personal: some people drink very little by preference, others find a drink now and then is fine. If you do drink, the NHS suggests [drinking water or a non-fizzy soft drink between alcoholic drinks and a glass of water before bed](https://111.wales.nhs.uk/hangover/), which also helps with the dehydration side.

## When should you talk to a health professional?

Talk to a doctor if your attacks are frequent, getting worse, or if you are unsure whether alcohol and your treatments mix well. Alcohol and medication can interact, and only a doctor or pharmacist who knows your situation can tell you what is right for you. If you take regular or preventive treatment, ask them directly. For more on what to expect from an appointment, see [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine).

Seek urgent help if you have a sudden, severe headache unlike anything before, or neurological symptoms such as weakness, trouble speaking or confusion. Our article on [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) explains the warning signs.

## What to remember about alcohol and migraine

Alcohol is a trigger for some people with migraine, but not for everyone, and rarely on its own. Red wine is the most blamed drink, though research does not show it is always the worst. Dehydration and a restless night often play a part, and stress around the occasion can be mistaken for the effect of the drink.

The most reliable way to know where you stand is to observe your own patterns kindly and over time, without guilt. If alcohol clearly bothers you, you are allowed to step back from it. If it does not, you do not need to give up everything "just in case". Either way, your doctor can help you decide what is right for you.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can one glass of wine trigger a migraine?

It can for some people, but not for most, and not every time. In [a large survey](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30565341/), even among people who blamed red wine, it consistently led to an attack in only 8.8% of participants. How you slept and how stressed you are can change how you react.

### Why do I get a migraine after drinking even a little?

It may be the drink itself, but also dehydration, a poor night's sleep or stress around the occasion. [The American Migraine Foundation notes](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/top-10-migraine-triggers/) that a study of 307 people found a link between stressful events and attacks, but not between alcohol and attacks. Keeping a diary for a few weeks can show which factor comes up most often.

### Is a hangover headache the same as a migraine?

Not always. A hangover headache is mostly linked to dehydration and the after-effects of alcohol, and [usually passes within 24 hours](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/hangovers/symptoms-causes/syc-20373012). A migraine can start the same way but often comes with one-sided throbbing pain, nausea, and sensitivity to light and sound, and it can last longer. If you are unsure, note your symptoms and talk to your doctor.

### Is white wine, beer or vodka safer than red wine for migraine?

There is no proof that one drink is safe for everyone. [The American Migraine Foundation says](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/top-10-migraine-triggers/) that other types of alcohol can be just as likely, and sometimes more often, to be the culprit. What matters is your own pattern, which is why a diary is more useful than a general rule.
