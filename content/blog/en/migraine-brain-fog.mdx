You are halfway through a sentence and the word you need is simply gone. Or you read the same email four times and nothing sticks. Maybe the headache has not even started yet, or it ended yesterday and your head still feels stuffed with cotton: that is migraine brain fog.

That is **migraine brain fog**, and it is one of the most common "invisible" parts of a migraine. It is real, it is part of the biology of the attack, and it usually passes. This article explains when it shows up, what studies say about memory and concentration, and how to note it without any effort so your doctor can use it. It sits alongside our overview of [migraine symptoms](/blog/migraine-symptoms), which covers the other signs beyond head pain.

**In short**

- Brain fog (trouble focusing, finding words, remembering things) can appear before, during or after a migraine attack, according to the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-brain-fog/).
- Studies consistently show worse thinking performance during an attack, while results between attacks are mixed, according to [a review in The Journal of Headache and Pain, indexed on PubMed Central](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6755588/).
- The same review found no sign of progressive cognitive decline over time in people with migraine.
- Jotting down when the fog starts and ends, in a diary or app, gives your doctor far more useful information than "my memory is bad".

## What is migraine brain fog, exactly?

Brain fog is not a medical diagnosis. It is a **everyday word for a group of thinking difficulties**: trouble staying focused, slowed thinking, forgetfulness, feeling less alert, or struggling to find the right word. The American Migraine Foundation says people with migraine often describe a [hard time staying focused or finding words](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-brain-fog/).

Researchers call these "cognitive symptoms". The word "cognitive" simply means anything to do with thinking: attention, memory, language, planning, and how fast you process information.

Brain fog is also different from a bad night's sleep or a busy week, even though it can feel similar. The difference is the pattern: it tends to follow the rhythm of your attacks rather than a random bad day. That pattern is exactly what is worth noticing.

## When can brain fog show up during a migraine attack?

Brain fog can appear in any phase of an attack, and sometimes more than one. A migraine attack is often described in up to four phases, and thinking difficulties have been linked to several of them.

### Before the headache (prodrome)

The prodrome is the early warning phase, which can start hours or even a day or two before the pain. The [Cleveland Clinic lists difficulty concentrating](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/5005-migraine-headaches) among the possible prodrome symptoms, alongside fatigue, neck stiffness and sensitivity to light or sound. If you often feel "off" the day before an attack, our guide to [migraine prodrome symptoms](/blog/migraine-prodrome-symptoms) lists the other early signs.

### During an aura

An aura is a short burst of temporary symptoms that usually builds over several minutes and fades within about an hour. It is mostly known for visual changes, but the [Cleveland Clinic notes it can also include difficulty speaking or concentrating](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/5005-migraine-headaches). Trouble finding words can therefore be a **language aura**, not just tiredness. Our article on [migraine with aura](/blog/migraine-with-aura) explains the different forms, and [aura beyond vision](/blog/migraine-aura-tingling-numbness-speech) covers speech and sensory symptoms in more detail.

### During the headache itself

Pain, nausea and sensitivity to light and noise all compete for your brain's attention, so it is not surprising that thinking slows down. A conference abstract indexed on PubMed Central reported that [cognitive symptoms during an attack were rated second only to pain](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4181047/) in how much they affected people. That is a small, early piece of evidence, but it matches what many people describe.

### After the pain ends (postdrome)

Many people feel wrung out once the headache goes. The Cleveland Clinic describes [mental fog and difficulty concentrating in the postdrome](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/migraine-hangover-postdrome), the "migraine hangover", which can last up to 48 hours. The American Migraine Foundation also says brain fog can [last up to 24 hours after the pain ends](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-brain-fog/). More on this phase in our article on the [migraine hangover](/blog/migraine-hangover-postdrome).

## What do studies say about memory and concentration?

The clearest finding is that thinking performance drops during an attack. A review of the research states that [all objective studies show some degree of impairment during an attack](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6755588/). The areas involved include attention, memory, processing speed and executive function, which is the set of skills you use to plan, switch tasks and make decisions.

Between attacks, the picture is less clear. According to the same review, [most clinic-based studies found worse performance than in healthy controls, but a few found no difference](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6755588/). In other words, scientists do not fully agree yet, and results may depend on who was studied (people seen in specialist clinics versus people in the general population).

There is also reassuring news. The review notes that [longitudinal studies, which follow people over time, do not suggest a progressive cognitive decline](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6755588/) in migraine. So temporary fog during and around attacks does not, on current evidence, mean your memory is steadily getting worse. If you are worried about that, it is a fair question to bring to your doctor.

## Why do words disappear during a migraine?

Nobody knows the full mechanism yet, but the leading idea is that migraine changes how parts of the brain work, including areas that handle language and attention. The American Migraine Foundation says that [brain fog is believed to be linked to a wave of reduced activity in the cortex, called cortical spreading depression](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-brain-fog/), in the lead-up to an attack, though it stresses that more research is needed.

Cortical spreading depression is the electrical wave also thought to underlie aura. That fits with the idea that finding words can be hard during or around an aura. Fog can also happen **without any aura**, so it is probably more than one mechanism at work.

In practical terms, it is the same brain that processes pain and sensory input that is also asked to find words. When it is busy or temporarily disturbed, language is one of the first things to feel the strain. That does not mean you are not trying hard enough.

## Does treating the migraine help the fog?

Often the most direct way to ease the fog is to reduce the attacks themselves. The American Migraine Foundation says the [best way to treat brain fog is to reduce migraine symptoms](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-brain-fog/), and recommends talking to your doctor about it if it happens before or after attacks.

The review also points out that [acute attack treatments do not always relieve the cognitive symptoms](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6755588/). So if your headache eases but your head still feels foggy, that is a known pattern, not a sign you are imagining it. What to take, or whether to change anything, is a decision for you and your doctor. If you are curious about the options, our guides to [migraine medication](/blog/migraine-medication) and [migraine prevention medication](/blog/migraine-prevention-medication) explain them in general terms.

## How can you note brain fog without effort?

The easiest method is to write down **three tiny things**: when the fog started, when it cleared, and what was going on around it. You do not need to describe it well. A few words is enough, and you can do it from your phone.

Here is a simple, low-effort way to do it:

- **Note the start.** "Foggy since morning, no pain yet" is gold for spotting a prodrome.
- **Note the words.** "Could not find words in a meeting" or "kept re-reading the same line" is more useful than "bad memory".
- **Note the end.** "Fog gone by lunch the day after" shows how long the postdrome lasts for you.
- **Note the context.** Poor sleep, a skipped meal, stress or your period can all be part of the story.

Our article on [what to write in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) goes through the other fields that help most. Once you have a few weeks of entries, you can start to see whether the fog arrives before the pain, or lingers after it, and how it relates to your triggers.

## How do you talk to your doctor about brain fog?

Bring examples and timing rather than a general feeling. Saying "for about a day before most attacks I can't concentrate, and after, I'm slow for another day" is much clearer than "I forget things".

It also helps to mention anything that changes the picture: [how many migraine days you have a month](/blog/how-many-migraines-a-month-is-normal), what you take and when, how well you sleep, and any mood changes. Low mood, anxiety and poor sleep can all affect concentration, so your doctor will want the full context.

A short written summary saves time in a short appointment. Our guide to the [migraine report for your doctor](/blog/migraine-report-for-doctor) shows what to include, and the article on [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine) explains who to book and how to prepare.

## When should you get help more quickly?

Sudden problems with speech or memory, especially with weakness or a drooping face, need urgent help, not a diary entry. The NHS advises calling 999 (or your local emergency number) for [sudden slurred speech, confusion, face drooping or arm weakness](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/stroke/symptoms/), and says to seek help even if symptoms pass quickly. It also lists [a sudden, extremely painful headache with speech or memory problems](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/subarachnoid-haemorrhage/) as an emergency.

If you have had migraine with aura for years and recognise your usual pattern, mild word-finding trouble that builds gradually and fades is typical. But if this feels new, different, or much more severe, do not wait. Our guide on [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) has more detail.

It is also worth booking a regular appointment if the fog is getting in the way of study or work, shows up outside your attacks, or gets steadily worse. Your doctor can check other possible causes, such as sleep, mood or other health conditions.

## What to take away

Brain fog is a genuine part of migraine for many people, and it can show up before, during and after the pain. Studies agree it is clear during attacks, less clear between them, and they have not found progressive decline over time. You are not lazy, and you are not losing your mind.

The most useful thing you can do is give it a timeline. A few words per attack, a few weeks in a row, can turn a vague complaint into something your doctor can work with.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can migraine cause temporary memory loss?

Yes, temporary memory problems are a recognised part of migraine for many people. The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-brain-fog/) says brain fog and temporary memory loss can come before, during or after an attack. These symptoms usually fade as the attack ends.

### Is migraine brain fog permanent?

Current evidence suggests not. A [review of cognitive dysfunction in migraine](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6755588/) reports that studies following people over time do not suggest progressive cognitive decline. If your symptoms are getting steadily worse or last beyond your attacks, tell your doctor.

### How long does brain fog last after a migraine?

It varies from person to person. The American Migraine Foundation says brain fog can [last up to 24 hours after the pain ends](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-brain-fog/), and the Cleveland Clinic says the [postdrome can last up to 48 hours](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/migraine-hangover-postdrome).

### Can brain fog be a warning sign that a migraine is coming?

For some people, yes. The Cleveland Clinic lists [difficulty concentrating as a possible prodrome symptom](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/5005-migraine-headaches). Noting when the fog starts, and whether pain follows, can show you whether it is an early signal for you.

### Should I see a doctor about brain fog with migraine?

It is worth mentioning it, especially if it affects your work or studies, happens outside your attacks, or is new or worsening. Bring dates, timing and examples. For sudden speech or memory problems with weakness or a drooping face, seek emergency help straight away.
