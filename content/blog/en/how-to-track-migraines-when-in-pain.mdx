You're lying in a dark room, the light from your phone feels like a needle, and the words you need won't come. Knowing how to track migraines is the last thing on your mind, and the last thing you want is to fill in a long form. Yet this is exactly the moment when the most useful details exist.

The good news: **how to track migraines** on a bad day is much simpler than most diary templates suggest. You only need a few seconds and a handful of facts during the attack, then a short top-up once your head is clearer. This article shows what to keep, what to leave for later, and how it all becomes a clear summary for your doctor. If you want the bigger picture on daily life with migraine, it sits in our [Living with migraine](/blog/living-with-migraine) section.

**In short**

- During an attack, note only four things: when it started, how strong it is, what you took (and when), and one or two symptoms such as light, noise or nausea.
- Use the lowest-effort format you can: a dimmed screen, a few taps, a voice note or a pen by the bed.
- The next day, add the end time, what helped, and what happened in the 24 hours before.
- A simple log kept over a few months is enough to give your doctor a clear picture, even if some days are missing.

## Why is a tiny note during the attack worth more than a perfect diary later?

A short note made in the moment captures details that fade within hours. Pain, nausea and brain fog make it hard to remember afterwards when the attack began, how bad it got, or what you took first.

Guidance from the [American Migraine Foundation](https://www.migrainedisorders.org/how-to-track-your-migraine-attacks/) explains that a diary can help you count your attacks, reveal patterns or possible triggers, and record how many days you use acute medication (the treatment you take to stop an attack). None of that needs essays. It needs reliable, repeatable basics.

The [Migraine Trust](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/self-management/keeping-a-migraine-diary/) also says it is often best to keep a headache diary simple and record basic information, such as the date, the day of the week and how long the attack lasted. In other words, a short log you actually keep beats a detailed one you abandon after a week.

## What are the only things worth noting when you hurt?

When you hurt, note four things: the start time, the strength of the pain, any medication and the time you took it, and a quick symptom tag. That is your whole job during the attack. Everything else can wait.

Here is what each one gives you:

- **Start time.** A rough time is fine ("around 7 am", "after lunch"). It helps you work out how long attacks last and when they tend to arrive. If you want a reference point, see [how long a migraine lasts](/blog/how-long-does-a-migraine-last).
- **Strength.** Use a tiny scale. Many headache clinics use something like none, mild, moderate or severe (0 to 3), and a simple scale is easier than "7 out of 10" when you can barely think.
- **What you took and when.** Write the name of the medication and the time, for example "ibuprofen, 8:15". This tells your doctor whether your treatment is working, and how often you need it. Don't worry about anything else here.
- **One or two symptom tags.** Light, sound, nausea, aura (visual or other sensory changes before or during the headache), dizziness. The [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/5005-migraine-headaches) lists sensitivity to light and sound as common symptoms during the headache phase, along with difficulty concentrating or speaking, so these are exactly the signs worth tagging.

That's it. If even this feels like too much, note the start time and nothing else. A single data point is better than a blank day.

## How can you note it when light hurts and words won't come?

Make the note as physical and as wordless as possible: fewer words, less light, fewer decisions. The aim is to set things up on a good day so that the bad day takes almost no thinking.

A few ideas you can try:

- **Dim everything.** Turn your phone brightness to the minimum and use dark mode if your phone has it. If screens are impossible, keep a pen and a small notebook by the bed. Our article on [why light, noise and smells bother you so much during migraine](/blog/migraine-light-sensitivity) explains why this happens.
- **Use a personal shorthand.** Pick letters or symbols once, when you feel well: L for light, S for sound, N for nausea, A for aura, D for dizzy. Then your note is "7:30, 2, L N, ibu 8:00". No sentences needed.
- **Talk instead of typing.** A short voice message to yourself, or to a partner, friend or flatmate, can work when reading and writing don't. Just say the time and the symptoms.
- **Ask someone to jot it down.** If someone is with you, they can write "started 7:30, took tablet 8:00" for you.
- **Skip the perfect wording.** If the right word won't come, write something close or leave it blank. Word-finding trouble is a recognised migraine symptom, and we cover it in [migraine brain fog](/blog/migraine-brain-fog). Your future self will understand a messy note.

## What should you add the next day, when your head is clearer?

On the day after, add the end time, what helped, and the context around the attack. Do this within a day or two if you can, before the details blur. This is the "calm" part of the diary and it can take about two minutes.

A short checklist for the top-up:

1. **End time.** When did the pain actually ease? This gives you the real duration.
2. **Peak strength.** How bad did it get at its worst?
3. **Impact.** Could you work, study or look after yourself, or did you need to lie down? The Mayo Clinic's advice for appointments points to tracking how your attacks affect you, and the [Migraine Trust](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/self-management/keeping-a-migraine-diary/) notes that diaries help you and your doctor work out what your triggers might be.
4. **What helped or didn't.** Medication, rest, a dark room, a cold pack, sleep.
5. **The 24 to 48 hours before.** Poor sleep, a skipped meal, a stressful day, your period, a weather change, lots of screen time. Mark these as possibilities, not proof. For more on this, read [how to identify your migraine triggers](/blog/identify-migraine-triggers).
6. **Warning signs and aftermath.** Did you feel yawny, irritable or thirsty beforehand? Were you wiped out the day after? These phases are covered in [migraine prodrome symptoms](/blog/migraine-prodrome-symptoms) and [the migraine hangover](/blog/migraine-hangover-postdrome).

If you want ideas about which extra details are worth the effort, [what to write down in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) goes through them one by one. You don't need all of it. Pick two or three extras and stick with them.

## Should you note mild attacks and good days too?

Yes, if you can: a one-line note for mild attacks and a quick "no migraine" on good days make your log far more useful. A diary with only the worst days can make things look better or worse than they really are.

You don't have to fully fill in these days. A single tap or a short mark is enough, such as "mild, no medication" or "clear". This also helps you spot how often you use pain medication, which is something your doctor will want to know. If you are curious about where your own numbers sit, see [how many migraines a month is normal](/blog/how-many-migraines-a-month-is-normal). And if you take painkillers often, our guide to [medication overuse headache](/blog/medication-overuse-headache) explains why the count matters.

## How do you turn these notes into a summary for your doctor?

Turn your notes into a one-page summary with how often attacks happen, how long they last, how strong they are, what you take and what seems to set them off. You don't have to hand over every entry.

The [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207) advises bringing your diary to your appointment so you can refer to it, and listing past treatments, including those that didn't work, with how long you took them, any side effects and how well they helped. The [Migraine Trust](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/self-management/keeping-a-migraine-diary/) suggests that keeping a diary for three months or more helps you and your doctor work out what your triggers might be.

Before you go, count and note:

- how many migraine days you had each month
- the usual length and strength of an attack
- how many days you took acute medication
- the most common symptoms (aura, nausea, light and sound sensitivity)
- any patterns, such as around your period, at weekends or after bad sleep

Our guide to a [migraine report for your doctor](/blog/migraine-report-for-doctor) shows what to put in a history summary, and [how to read your calendar, duration and intensity](/blog/migraine-diary-patterns) helps you spot trends before the appointment. If you are not sure who to book, see [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine). Treatment choices are always a conversation with your doctor, not something a diary can decide.

## What if you forget to note, or you give up for a while?

Missing days are normal and don't ruin your log. A diary with gaps still shows rough patterns, and you can start again at any time, with no need to "catch up".

A few ways to make it easier to restart:

- **Make the first step tiny.** Aim for one thing per attack, such as the start time. You can build up later.
- **Fill in later from memory.** If you missed an attack, write what you remember and mark it "approx".
- **Pick one place.** Choose a notebook, a notes app or a tracker, and stick with it so you aren't hunting for your notes.
- **Choose a tool that fits your worst days.** Apps differ a lot in how many steps they ask for. Our [guide to choosing a migraine app](/blog/best-migraine-app) lists ten criteria, and speed during an attack is a big one.

If tracking starts to feel like a chore or a source of guilt, it's fine to scale back to "start time and medication" for a few weeks. The log is there to help you, not to grade you.

## When is a note not the priority?

Sometimes your health comes first and the diary can wait. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/symptoms/) says to call 999 if you have problems speaking or remembering things, weakness or being unable to move on one side of your body or face, a sudden and extremely painful headache, loss of vision or double vision, drowsiness or confusion, or a seizure. It also says to call 111 if an attack lasts longer than 72 hours or aura symptoms last longer than an hour at a time.

Those are UK numbers, so use your local emergency number elsewhere. For a closer look, read [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine). If a symptom is new or different from your usual attacks, get it checked and do the notes afterwards.

## A small log that survives your worst days

A good migraine log is the one you can still keep when you feel awful. Four quick facts during the attack (start, strength, medication, one or two symptoms) and a two-minute top-up the next day are enough to see patterns and to give your doctor a clear summary.

Set up your shorthand while you feel well, keep your tool within reach, and accept gaps. If you want more guidance on everyday life with attacks, you'll find it in our [Living with migraine](/blog/living-with-migraine) section.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the minimum I should write down during a migraine?

The start time and what you took, with the time, is the bare minimum. If you have the energy, add a pain level and one or two symptoms such as light sensitivity or nausea. The Migraine Trust advises keeping a headache diary simple and focusing on basic details like date and duration.

### Should I track my migraines on paper or in an app?

Either works. The American Migraine Foundation says a smartphone app suits people who are always on their phone, while a paper calendar works just as well for others. On bad days, choose whichever needs fewer steps and less screen light, and you can switch if one stops working.

### How soon after an attack should I fill in the rest?

Ideally within a day or two, while the details are still fresh. There is no strict rule, and an approximate entry is more useful than none. Mark anything you are unsure about as "approx".

### Do I need to keep a diary for my doctor to take me seriously?

No, but it helps. The Mayo Clinic advises bringing your diary to your appointment, and a few months of simple notes can show how often attacks happen and how well your treatment works. Your own description still matters, even if your log has gaps.
