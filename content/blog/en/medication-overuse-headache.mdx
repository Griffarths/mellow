You wake up with a dull headache, so you take a painkiller. It helps for a few hours. Then the headache comes back, and so does the tablet. After a few weeks, you realise you're reaching for the pack almost every day, and the headaches seem to be coming more often, not less. This pattern has a name: medication overuse headache.

If that sounds familiar, you may be hearing about **medication overuse headache**, also called rebound headache. It's a real and well-recognised pattern, and it's more common than most people think. It isn't your fault, and it can be turned around, usually with help from a doctor.

In this article, we'll look at how it works, how often is "too often", the signs worth noticing, and why stopping on your own isn't the best plan. This is general information, not a diagnosis, and it doesn't replace advice from your doctor. For the wider picture on treatment options, you can also browse our [migraine treatments hub](/blog/migraine-treatments).

**In short**

- Medication overuse headache is a headache pattern that can develop when pain relief is used too often, in someone who already has a headache condition such as migraine ([Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20377083)).
- Experts say the risk rises with frequent use over many days a month, and some medicines, such as triptans and those containing butalbital, carry a higher risk ([Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20377083)).
- Headaches usually get worse before they get better when the medicine is reduced, which is why a doctor's plan matters ([Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20377089)).
- It's treatable: about half of people with chronic migraine improve significantly after stopping an overused acute medication, according to the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/what-is-medication-overuse-headache/).

## What is medication overuse headache?

Medication overuse headache is a headache that appears or gets worse because you've been taking headache medicine too often. The Cleveland Clinic explains that it happens when you [treat headaches with medication too often, causing more headaches](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/6170-rebound-headaches), which can sometimes be even worse than the original ones.

It mainly affects people who already have a headache condition. The Cleveland Clinic notes that those most at risk have [primary headache disorders such as migraine, cluster or tension-type headache](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/6170-rebound-headaches) that aren't well controlled. In other words, it's rarely about someone being careless. It's usually about someone trying to get through the day with painful, frequent attacks.

It's not rare, either. The World Health Organization describes it as the [most common secondary headache disorder, affecting up to 5% of some populations, and women more than men](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/headache-disorders). (A "secondary" headache is one caused by something else, here the medicine pattern. If a term is unfamiliar, our [migraine glossary](/blog/migraine-glossary) explains it in plain language.)

## How do painkillers keep headaches going?

Honestly, nobody fully knows. Mayo Clinic says that [experts don't know exactly why medication overuse headaches happen](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20377083), even though the pattern itself is well documented.

What we do know is the shape of the cycle:

1. You have frequent migraine or headache attacks.
2. You take acute pain relief (the kind you use once a headache has started) often to cope.
3. Over weeks or months, the headaches become more frequent, and you need relief more often.
4. The extra days of medicine feed the cycle, and the underlying headache condition becomes harder to treat.

Mayo Clinic adds that medication overuse headache can [make the underlying headache condition difficult to treat](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20377083). So the cycle isn't just frustrating, it can also get in the way of the treatments that would help most.

Some other factors seem to play a role. Mayo Clinic says [smoking is linked to a higher risk, and daily caffeine may add to it too](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20377083).

## How often is "too often"?

Roughly speaking, using headache medicine on more than 10 to 15 days a month is the range where the risk goes up, and it depends on the type of medicine. This isn't about how much you take at once. It's about how many days each month you use something to treat headaches.

Here's how the main sources describe it:

- **Simple painkillers** such as aspirin or ibuprofen: Mayo Clinic says problems may occur [if you take them for more than 14 days a month](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20377083).
- **Triptans** (migraine-specific medicines): the same page says [more than nine days a month](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20377083) may be enough. You can read more in our guide to [triptans, their side effects and safety](/blog/triptans-explained).
- **Medicines containing butalbital**: Mayo Clinic lists these as [carrying a high risk](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20377083).
- **In general**: the American Migraine Foundation says any acute medication can cause the problem [when used more than ten to fifteen days a month](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/what-is-medication-overuse-headache/).

These are general thresholds, not personal targets. Your own situation, including which medicines you use and any health conditions, is something only your doctor or pharmacist can assess. If you want a neutral comparison of what's in the cupboard, see our article on [paracetamol, ibuprofen and aspirin for headache](/blog/painkiller-for-headache).

One more detail that surprises many people: the days add up across everything you take. Counting a paracetamol (acetaminophen in the US) on Monday, an ibuprofen on Tuesday and a triptan on Wednesday, that's three medicine days, even if each tablet feels harmless.

## Which signs should make you stop and take notice?

The clearest sign is a change in your pattern: more headache days, more medicine days, and less relief. Mayo Clinic notes that symptoms depend on the type of headache and the medicine, but [your headache pattern changes](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20377083), and you may also have nausea.

Things worth noticing include:

- Headaches on 15 or more days a month for more than three months, while also using acute medicine regularly. This is how the formal definition works, as [described by Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20377089).
- Headaches that come back as the medicine wears off, so you take another one.
- Waking up with a headache more and more often.
- Feeling that your usual medicine "doesn't work like it used to".
- Worrying about running out, or planning your week around having tablets with you.

None of these proves you have medication overuse headache. They are simply good reasons to talk to someone. And if your treatment doesn't seem to be working, [migraine medication not working](/blog/migraine-medication-not-working) covers timing, nausea and other common reasons.

## Why does counting headache days and medicine days help?

Because it turns a vague feeling ("I think I take a lot") into clear numbers you and your doctor can work with. Doctors can often reach a diagnosis from your headache history and your medicine use. Mayo Clinic says [most people don't need any tests](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20377089), so your own records carry real weight.

A simple tally for a month or two is enough. Note down:

- the days you had a headache and roughly how strong it was,
- the days you took anything for it, including over-the-counter products,
- what you took, and whether it helped,
- other possible influences such as sleep, stress or your cycle.

Our guide on [what to write in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) walks through this, and you can turn your notes into a clear summary with [a migraine report for your doctor](/blog/migraine-report-for-doctor). If your attacks are frequent, it can also help to understand [where the 15-day line sits between episodic and chronic migraine](/blog/chronic-migraine-vs-episodic-migraine).

## Why shouldn't you stop painkillers on your own?

Because the first weeks can be rough, and a plan made with your doctor makes it safer and more likely to work. Mayo Clinic is straightforward about this: [when you stop your medicine, expect headaches to get worse before they get better](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20377089). Many people give up in that stretch and go back to the tablets, which restarts the cycle.

There are other reasons to involve a professional:

- **Some medicines need a supervised approach.** The American Migraine Foundation says you should [work with your doctor to come off certain medications, such as opioids or butalbital-containing medicines, safely](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/what-is-medication-overuse-headache/).
- **Your underlying migraine still needs a plan.** If you stop everything with nothing in its place, the attacks that started this are still there.
- **Not every frequent headache is medication overuse.** A doctor can check whether something else is going on.

So the message isn't "never take pain relief". It's that if you suspect a pattern, the safest first step is to **talk to your doctor or pharmacist rather than stopping or changing things by yourself**. Our guide on [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine) explains who to book and how to prepare.

## What can a doctor offer to break the cycle?

Usually a combination: a plan to reduce the problem medicine, support through the difficult weeks, and a treatment to calm the underlying migraine. Mayo Clinic says that to break the cycle you'll need to [stop or cut down on pain medicine](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20377089), and that your healthcare professional may prescribe treatments to ease the pain and the withdrawal effects.

Options your doctor may discuss include:

- **Preventive medicine.** Mayo Clinic says preventive medicines [may help you break the cycle and ease the underlying headache condition](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20377089), either during or after withdrawal.
- **Botox injections.** Mayo Clinic notes that onabotulinumtoxinA [may lower the number of headaches you have each month and make them less severe](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20377089). Our article on [Botox for chronic migraine](/blog/botox-for-chronic-migraine) explains how it's given.
- **Newer preventive options.** Our guide to [CGRP inhibitors](/blog/cgrp-migraine-treatment) explains a class of preventive treatments designed specifically for migraine. Whether they suit you is a question for your doctor.
- **Non-drug support.** The American Migraine Foundation mentions [mindfulness-based stress reduction, relaxation techniques and cognitive behavioural therapy](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/what-is-medication-overuse-headache/) as ways to help calm your reaction to a migraine. For ideas you can try alongside medical care, see [how to relieve a migraine without medication](/blog/relieve-migraine-without-medication).

The outlook is encouraging. The American Migraine Foundation says [about half of patients with chronic migraine significantly improve after stopping an overused acute medication](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/what-is-medication-overuse-headache/). That leaves others who need more time or a different approach, which is another reason to keep your doctor involved.

## When should you get medical help without waiting?

Book an appointment if you're using headache medicine on many days each month, if your headaches are becoming more frequent, or if your usual pain relief is no longer working. You don't need to wait for a "bad enough" moment.

Seek urgent care for headaches that feel different from anything you've had before. That includes a sudden, severe headache that peaks within moments, or a headache with weakness, confusion, trouble speaking, fever with a stiff neck, or vision changes you don't recognise. Our guide on [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) lays out the warning signs in more detail.

## In short: a cycle that breaks with support

Medication overuse headache is a pattern, not a personal failing. It tends to develop when frequent attacks push you to treat them again and again, and it can make the underlying migraine harder to manage.

The practical steps are simple. Track your headache days and medicine days, notice whether your pattern is changing, and bring those notes to your doctor or pharmacist. Avoid stopping everything alone, because the early weeks can be hard and a plan makes a real difference. With the right support, many people find their headaches become more manageable.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can paracetamol or ibuprofen really cause more headaches?

Yes, if they are used on too many days each month, in someone who already has a headache condition. Mayo Clinic says problems may occur [with aspirin or ibuprofen taken more than 14 days a month](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20377083). It's the frequency of use over time that matters, so ask your doctor or pharmacist what's appropriate for you.

### How long does medication overuse headache last?

It varies from person to person, and your doctor can give you a realistic timeline. Mayo Clinic says headaches typically [get worse before they get better](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20377089) after the medicine is reduced. Many people then see improvement, and the American Migraine Foundation reports that [about half of people with chronic migraine improve significantly](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/what-is-medication-overuse-headache/).

### Is rebound headache the same as medication overuse headache?

Yes, they describe the same thing. The Cleveland Clinic says medication overuse headache is the formal name, while [rebound headache is the more commonly used term](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/6170-rebound-headaches).

### Do I need a scan to find out?

Usually not. Mayo Clinic says healthcare professionals can typically diagnose it [based on your history of headaches and your use of medicine](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/medication-overuse-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20377089), and most people don't need testing. A doctor may still order tests if something else is suspected.
