You have been logging your attacks for months. Now you finally have an appointment with a new GP, a neurologist or a headache specialist, and you are wondering how to build a migraine report for doctor visits from all those notes. Do you hand over a phone full of entries and hope for the best, or try to explain everything from memory in ten minutes?

Neither is ideal. A good **migraine report for doctor visits fits on one page**: a short summary of how often you get attacks, how long and how bad they are, what you take, and what seems to set them off. This article shows you, step by step, how to turn months of diary entries into that page. If you want the bigger picture on managing life with migraine, our [Living with migraine](/blog/living-with-migraine) section is a good place to start.

**In short**

- A simple one-page summary is usually more useful to a doctor than a long diary: the [Migraine Trust](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/healthcare/tips-to-help-you-advocate-for-your-health/) says there is no need to give your GP lots of data, and that a simple summary is ideally enough.
- Cover the last 3 months or so, count attack days and headache days per month, and give typical duration and intensity (for example on a 0 to 10 scale) as ranges, not averages alone.
- List your medicines separately for attacks you treated and attacks you did not, with how well each worked and how many days per month you take them.
- Mention possible triggers as patterns you noticed, not as proven causes, and add how migraine affects your daily life plus your questions.

## Why summarize your diary instead of showing all of it?

A summary gets to the point quickly, and appointments are short. Your doctor needs to see the pattern, not read 60 entries one by one.

The Migraine Trust, a UK charity, advises that you [don't need to give your GP lots of data](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/healthcare/tips-to-help-you-advocate-for-your-health/) and that a simple summary is ideally enough. It also suggests explaining briefly how migraine affects your day-to-day life.

That does not mean your detailed diary is useless. Keep it with you in case your doctor wants to look at a specific attack. Mayo Clinic suggests [bringing your diary to the appointment](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207) so you can refer to it, and even asking whether there is anything else you should track. Think of the summary as the cover page and the diary as the appendix.

If you are still building your diary, our guide on [what to write down in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) covers the basics.

## What should your migraine report for doctor visits cover?

Your migraine report for doctor appointments should cover the last 3 months or so, and include every headache day, not just the worst attacks. A short window shows your current pattern, and a few months smooth out one unusually good or bad month.

For a first appointment, the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/diagnosis/) says it can be useful to keep a diary for a few weeks, and that a GP needs to identify a pattern of recurring headaches and symptoms to reach a diagnosis. So if you only have a few weeks of notes, bring those. A shorter record is still much better than none.

A few choices will make your summary clearer:

- **Include milder days.** A dull headache that never became a full attack still counts when you tally headache days.
- **Include attacks you treated and attacks you did not.** Both tell the doctor something, and we will come back to this below.
- **Say what you count as one attack.** If a headache comes back the next morning, decide whether it is one long attack or two, and mention your rule.
- **Flag changes.** A new job, a new contraceptive, a house move or a stressful period can explain a change in your pattern.

Not sure whether you are seeing a GP, a neurologist or someone else? Our guide on [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine) explains who to book and how to prepare.

## How do you summarize frequency, duration and intensity?

Count your days per month, then give the typical duration and intensity as a range. Three short lines are enough for most people.

### Frequency: attack days and headache days

The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/talking-to-your-doctor-about-migraine/) notes that useful diary information includes the number of migraine attacks and days with headache in the previous month. So write one line per month, for example: "March: 5 migraine days, 3 other headache days."

Why does this matter? Doctors often think in terms of headache days. The international classification defines chronic migraine as at least 15 headache days per month, with at least 8 of them being migraine days, for more than 3 months. The American Migraine Foundation points out that this [15-day cutoff is debated](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/defining-chronic-migraine/), since people with 8 to 14 migraine days a month can be just as disabled. You do not need to label yourself, just give honest counts. Our article on [chronic vs episodic migraine](/blog/chronic-migraine-vs-episodic-migraine) explains the difference.

### Duration: from first sign to the end

Write the shortest, the longest and the usual length of your attacks, in hours or days. For example: "Usually 12 to 24 hours, longest 3 days."

Decide whether you count the warning phase before the pain and the wiped-out feeling afterwards, and say so in a few words. If you are curious about what is normal, see [how long a migraine lasts](/blog/how-long-does-a-migraine-last).

### Intensity: a simple scale

The American Migraine Foundation's guidance on [headache journals](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/headache-journals/) suggests rating severity on a 0 to 10 scale. The Migraine Trust also accepts simple words such as mild, moderate or severe.

Give a range and a typical value: "Most attacks 6 to 8 out of 10, two attacks 9 out of 10." Then add how many attacks forced you to stop what you were doing, which is often more telling than the number itself. To learn how to read these patterns yourself first, see our guide to [migraine diary patterns](/blog/migraine-diary-patterns).

## How do you present your medication, treated and untreated attacks?

Show your medicines in two groups: attacks where you took something, and attacks where you did not or could not. This is the part of the summary doctors tend to look at closely, because it shows what has already been tried.

For each medicine you use during an attack, note:

1. The name of the medicine (brand or generic, whichever is on the box).
2. How many attacks you used it for in the period.
3. How well it worked: fully, partly or not at all, and roughly how long it took.
4. How early in the attack you took it.
5. Any side effects, such as nausea, sleepiness or a racing heart.

The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/talking-to-your-doctor-about-migraine/) recommends bringing a list of all treatments you have tried before, including how you responded to each and any side effects. [Mayo Clinic Press](https://mcpress.mayoclinic.org/migraine/migraine-treatment-what-to-tell-your-neurologist/) likewise advises writing down all the medicines, vitamins and supplements you take, with a particular emphasis on those you use for headaches. Put doses in that list exactly as written on your packaging or prescription, and never guess.

### Count your medication days

Add one line with the number of days per month you took any acute medicine, meaning something you take to treat an attack. The American Migraine Foundation explains that [medication overuse headache](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/2203_AMF_Medication_Overuse_V5_Digital.pdf) is linked to using simple pain relievers on 15 or more days per month, and combination pain relievers or triptans on 10 or more days per month. It also notes that the risk can start at a higher or lower number depending on the person.

You do not have to work out whether this applies to you. Just give the number and let your doctor interpret it.

### Do not forget the attacks you did not treat

Write down the attacks where you took nothing, and why: it was mild, you could not keep a pill down, you were at work, you worried about taking too much. This gives a more honest picture than a list of medicines alone. If your treatments have stopped working, our article on [why migraine medication may not be working](/blog/migraine-medication-not-working) covers common reasons, and [this overview of migraine medication](/blog/migraine-medication) explains the main families of treatment. Your doctor is the right person to decide what, if anything, to change.

## Which triggers belong in the summary without overinterpreting?

Include the two or three patterns you noticed most often, and describe them as "possible triggers", not as causes. Migraine triggers are tricky: what looks like a trigger can sometimes be an early symptom, like craving sweets before an attack, and many people react to a combination of factors rather than one thing.

Good wording sounds like this:

- "Poor sleep the night before: 6 of 11 attacks."
- "Attacks cluster around the first 2 days of my period."
- "Stressful deadlines at work, then an attack once things calm down."

The NHS suggests noting [what you were doing when the migraine began](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/diagnosis/), and the Mayo Clinic mentions asking your doctor whether [any medication you take, including birth control pills](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207), could trigger or worsen migraine.

Try to add the reverse as well: "I slept badly on many nights without getting an attack." This stops a single pattern from looking stronger than it is. Our guide on [how to identify your migraine triggers](/blog/identify-migraine-triggers) goes deeper into this.

## What else should you add: impact and your questions?

Add two short sections: how migraine affects your life, and the questions you want answered. These are what turn a list of numbers into a conversation.

The Migraine Trust lists [impact on your life and day-to-day activities](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/healthcare/tips-to-help-you-advocate-for-your-health/) as something to tell your GP. Be concrete: days of work or school missed, plans cancelled, time lying in a dark room, nights disturbed. Two or three lines are enough.

Mayo Clinic also suggests writing down your symptoms, major stresses or recent life changes, and questions for the doctor. Possible questions include:

- Could you look at my diary, and is there anything else I should track?
- Could any of my current medicines or my contraception affect my migraines?
- What are my options for treating attacks and for preventing them?
- When should I come back, and what should I do if things change?

If you have a family member or friend who can come with you, that can help you remember what was said.

## What does a complete one-page summary look like?

A complete summary has six short blocks: period, frequency, duration and intensity, medication, possible triggers, and impact plus questions. Here is an example with made-up numbers, which you can adapt.

**Migraine summary, \[your name], \[date]**

**Period covered:** June to August (3 months).

**Frequency:** June 5 migraine days and 2 other headache days. July 6 and 3. August 4 and 2. Migraine with visual aura in about 1 of 4 attacks.

**Duration and intensity:** Usually 12 to 24 hours, longest 3 days. Most attacks 6 to 8 out of 10, two at 9 out of 10. Nausea in most attacks, sensitivity to light and sound in all of them.

**Medication:** Painkiller A taken for 8 attacks, helped partly, works better when taken early. Triptan B taken for 4 attacks, worked fully in 3 and not at all in 1 (took it late). No medicine taken for 3 attacks (mild, or vomiting). Acute medicine taken on about 6 days per month. Previously tried: \[list, with results and side effects].

**Possible triggers:** Poor sleep before 6 of 11 attacks. Attacks around the first 2 days of my period in 2 of 3 cycles. Possibly stressful weeks, then relaxing.

**Impact:** 4 half-days of work missed, 3 social plans cancelled, 2 nights with disturbed sleep.

**Questions:** What can I do to prevent attacks? Is my treatment right for me? Should I have any tests?

That is about 200 words. If it fits on one page and you can read it in a minute, you have done it right. Do not worry about tests for now: our article on [MRI for migraine](/blog/mri-for-migraine) explains when a scan is, and is not, usually needed.

## How do you keep a history that is easy to summarize?

The easiest way is to log every attack the same way, right when it happens, rather than reconstructing it weeks later. Memory fades quickly, and the details that matter most (time of medicine, how long it took to work) are exactly the ones we forget.

A few habits help:

- **Pick a short list of fields** and stick to it: start time, end time, intensity, medicine and timing, possible triggers, and for some people the day of their cycle.
- **Log the mild days too**, even with one word, so your headache-day counts are right.
- **Do a quick monthly tally** at the end of the month while it is fresh, instead of 90 days at once.
- **Use whatever you will actually keep up with.** The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/headache-journals/) notes that people track with written journals, smartphone apps or spreadsheets.

If you prefer an app, our guide to the [best migraine app](/blog/best-migraine-app) lists 10 criteria for choosing one, including how easy it is to log an attack when you feel awful.

## Conclusion

A migraine report for doctor appointments does not have to be complicated. One page with your attack and headache days per month, typical duration and intensity, medicines for treated and untreated attacks, a few possible triggers, the impact on your life and your questions is usually enough to start a useful conversation.

Be honest rather than perfect. If your notes have gaps, say so, and bring what you have. Your doctor will take it from there, and this page is only a tool to help you be heard. Treatment decisions always belong to you and your healthcare professional.

## Frequently asked questions

### How far back should my migraine summary go?

About 3 months is a practical window for most appointments, because it shows a current pattern without being too long. If you only have a few weeks of notes, bring those: the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/diagnosis/) says a diary kept for a few weeks can already help with diagnosis. Mention any big changes outside that window, such as a new treatment or a change in contraception.

### Should I bring my whole diary or just the summary?

Bring both, but lead with the summary. The Migraine Trust says a simple summary is [ideally enough](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/healthcare/tips-to-help-you-advocate-for-your-health/), and Mayo Clinic suggests having your diary with you so you can [refer to it](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207) if your doctor asks about a specific attack.

### What if I forgot to log some attacks?

Say so, and give your best estimate with a note like "about 2 more mild headaches I did not record." Doctors are used to imperfect diaries. A clear, honest summary with a few gaps is better than none at all.

### Can I write down which medicine to take or change?

No, your summary should describe what you took and how it worked, not decide what comes next. Choosing, adjusting or stopping a treatment is a decision for your doctor or pharmacist, based on your full health history.

### Is a migraine report only useful for a first appointment?

No. It can also help at follow-up visits, to show whether the number of headache days, the intensity or your medication days have changed since last time. The American Migraine Foundation notes that diaries help you and your doctor [track how treatments are working](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/headache-journals/).
