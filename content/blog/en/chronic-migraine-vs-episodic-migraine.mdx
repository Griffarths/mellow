You had three migraines last month. This month it feels like you've had a headache on and off for two weeks, and you wonder if something has changed. Maybe a doctor once said the word "chronic" and it stuck in your head, and now you're asking yourself what chronic migraine vs episodic migraine really means for you.

The difference between **chronic migraine vs episodic migraine** comes down to one number: how many days a month you have headaches. Below that line it's called episodic, at or above it, chronic. It's a useful label for doctors, but it's not a verdict on you, and as you'll see, people move across that line in both directions.

This article explains what the 15-day rule really says, why it exists, how someone goes from one side to the other, and why a simple record of your attacks can make your next appointment much more useful. It sits alongside our overview of [migraine symptoms](/blog/migraine-symptoms), if you want the bigger picture.

**In short**

- Episodic migraine means fewer than 15 headache days a month, and chronic migraine means 15 or more headache days a month for more than three months, at least 8 of them with migraine features ([ICHD-3](https://ichd-3.org/1-migraine/1-3-chronic-migraine/)).
- The 15-day line is a convention, not a biological border: people with 8 to 14 migraine days a month can be just as burdened as those above the line ([American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/defining-chronic-migraine/)).
- About 3% of people with episodic migraine move to chronic migraine each year, and chronic migraine can also go back to episodic ([American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/understanding-chronic-migraine/), [Neurology 2011 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21270413/)).
- A record of your headache days, intensity and medication use gives your doctor a much clearer picture than memory alone.

## What is the definition of chronic migraine?

Chronic migraine means **headache on 15 or more days a month, for more than three months, with migraine features on at least 8 of those days**. That's the wording of the official classification used by doctors, the [International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3)](https://ichd-3.org/1-migraine/1-3-chronic-migraine/). The [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/9638-chronic-migraine) describes it the same way.

Three details matter here:

- **It counts headache days, not only migraine attacks.** A day with a milder, tension-like headache still counts toward the 15.
- **It has to last.** One bad month isn't chronic migraine. The pattern needs to hold for more than three months.
- **At least 8 days need migraine features.** That means the typical signs, such as one-sided throbbing pain, nausea, or sensitivity to light and sound, or a headache that responds to migraine-specific medicine. If some of the terms are unfamiliar, our [migraine glossary](/blog/migraine-glossary) explains them in plain language.

Chronic migraine isn't a different disease. According to the Cleveland Clinic, [the symptoms are the same as in episodic migraine](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/9638-chronic-migraine), they just happen more often.

## What is episodic migraine?

Episodic migraine is migraine with **fewer than 15 headache days a month**. It's the most common pattern: the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/understanding-chronic-migraine/) notes that episodic migraine is defined by fewer than 15 headache days a month, while chronic migraine starts at 15.

"Episodic" covers a huge range. For one person it's one attack every couple of months. For another it's ten or twelve migraine days a month, which is hard to live with but still technically episodic. That's why you'll sometimes hear doctors split it into "low-frequency" and "high-frequency" episodic migraine, even though the official classification doesn't.

How long each attack lasts is a separate question. If you want that detail, see [how long a migraine lasts](/blog/how-long-does-a-migraine-last).

## Why is the line set at 15 days?

The 15-day line is mostly **a practical convention agreed by experts**, not a point where the brain suddenly changes. Chronic migraine first got its own official criteria in the 2006 revision of the headache classification, which used 15 or more headache days a month for at least three months, with migraine features on at least 8 of them ([The Journal of Headache and Pain](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10194-011-0393-6)).

A clear cutoff helps in several ways. It lets researchers compare studies, it gives clinical trials a consistent group to recruit, and it helps decide which treatments a person may qualify for.

But the line has critics. The American Migraine Foundation points out that [the 15-day cutoff is under debate](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/defining-chronic-migraine/), because headache days naturally fluctuate from month to month. It also reports that people with 8 to 14 headache days a month can find their migraine just as disabling as people with 15 or more. A [2022 analysis in Pain and Therapy](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s40122-022-00375-z), using data from a registry built by the American Migraine Foundation, found similar burden (pain intensity, work productivity, anxiety and depression) in those two groups.

So the honest answer: the number is useful, but it's a tool. If you have 13 bad days a month, you don't need to wait for a 15 to deserve support.

## How common is chronic migraine?

Chronic migraine is much less common than episodic migraine, affecting **roughly 1.4 to 2.2% of the general population** according to [one review of the research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8915760/). Estimates vary between studies, depending on how people were surveyed and which definition was used. The American Migraine Foundation, for example, [cites a higher range for the US](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/what-is-chronic-migraine/).

What's consistent across the research is that most people with migraine are in the episodic group. If you have episodic migraine, you're in the large majority. And if you're in the chronic group, you're far from alone.

## How does episodic migraine become chronic?

Episodic migraine can slowly become chronic, but it's **not common in any given year and it's not inevitable**. The American Migraine Foundation says that [around 3% of people with episodic migraine transition to chronic migraine each year](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/understanding-chronic-migraine/). Researchers call this "progression" or "chronification".

A team of headache experts reviewed the evidence on what raises or lowers the chances of progression, grouping the factors into [five areas: migraine characteristics, treatment-related factors, other health conditions, lifestyle, and demographics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10632231/). Here is what studies have linked to a higher risk:

- **More frequent attacks.** A [meta-analysis](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20200107/Meta-analysis-highlights-predictors-of-chronic-migraine.aspx) found high-quality evidence that having 10 or more headache days a month was a strong predictor of progression.
- **Frequent use of acute medication.** Taking painkillers or triptans on many days a month can lead to medication overuse headache. In the classification criteria, [the thresholds are 10 or more days a month for triptans and 15 or more for simple painkillers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4959634). If this sounds like you, talk to your doctor before changing anything.
- **Other health conditions.** Depression, anxiety, sleep problems and obesity have all been associated with progression.
- **Lifestyle factors.** The same expert review lists [high caffeine intake, tobacco use and physical inactivity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10632231/) among the modifiable factors.

Two cautions. These are **associations, not proof of cause**: someone with more severe migraine may also be more likely to take more medicine, for example. And having a risk factor doesn't mean you'll progress. It simply points to areas you and your doctor can look at together.

The encouraging part is that it can go the other way too. In a [US study of people with chronic migraine followed over two years](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21270413/), about 26% went back to episodic migraine. The Cleveland Clinic gives a range of [26% to 70%](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/9638-chronic-migraine) and adds that some people move back and forth over time.

## What actually changes between episodic and chronic migraine?

In daily life, the main difference is **how much space migraine takes up**, not how a single attack feels. The attacks themselves look similar, but with chronic migraine there are fewer clear gaps between them, and the recovery phase of one attack (see [migraine hangover](/blog/migraine-hangover-postdrome)) can run into the start of the next.

Here is how the two compare:

| | Episodic migraine | Chronic migraine |
|---|---|---|
| Headache days per month | Fewer than 15 | 15 or more, for over 3 months |
| Migraine-feature days | Variable | At least 8 a month |
| Symptoms of an attack | Typical migraine symptoms | The same symptoms, more often |
| Can change over time? | Can progress to chronic | Can return to episodic |

Chronic migraine also tends to weigh more heavily on life. The Mayo Clinic notes that chronic daily headaches, the broader group that includes chronic migraine, are [among the most disabling headache conditions](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/chronic-daily-headaches/symptoms-causes/syc-20370891) because of their constant nature.

Treatment options also differ in places. Some preventive treatments are studied or approved specifically for chronic migraine. They include [Botox injections following the PREEMPT protocol](/blog/botox-for-chronic-migraine), and the Cleveland Clinic lists [Botox, nerve blocks and nerve stimulation](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/9638-chronic-migraine) among options. Newer drugs that target the CGRP pathway are covered in our [guide to CGRP inhibitors](/blog/cgrp-migraine-treatment). Whether any of them suits you is a decision for you and your doctor.

## What if I have a headache almost every day?

If you have a headache on most days, **it's worth booking a medical appointment rather than guessing the cause**. Daily or near-daily headaches can be chronic migraine, but they can also be tension-type headache, medication overuse headache, or a mix of several. Only a doctor can sort this out, and the [NHS advises seeing a GP](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/) if you have migraine attacks more than once a week or find them hard to control.

Our article on [why you may have a headache every day](/blog/headache-every-day) goes through the possible explanations. If your pain is a milder, band-like pressure, our page on [tension headache symptoms](/blog/tension-headache-symptoms) helps you compare the two.

Some signs need urgent care instead of a routine appointment, such as a sudden, severe "worst headache of your life" or new weakness or confusion. Our guide on [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) lists them.

## Why does the history of your attacks help your doctor so much?

A clear record turns "I get a lot of headaches" into **numbers your doctor can work with**. Memory is a poor tool here: bad days blur together, and people tend to remember the worst attack rather than the average month.

Your doctor wants to know things like:

- How many headache days you have each month, and how many have migraine features.
- How intense the attacks are and how long they last.
- How often you take acute medicine, and whether it works.
- What seems to come before attacks (sleep, stress, your cycle, food).
- Whether the pattern is stable, getting better or getting worse over several months.

Because the chronic label depends on a count over more than three months, a record is almost a requirement for an accurate answer. It also helps you see trends, such as a slow rise from 6 days to 9 to 12, before they cross any official line. Our guide to [reading your migraine diary patterns](/blog/migraine-diary-patterns) shows what to look for, and [what to write down in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) covers the basics.

If you're preparing for an appointment, our article on [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine) explains who to book and how to get ready. And if medicines aren't giving you the relief you expect, the record also helps you and your doctor look at timing and other reasons together; see [migraine medication not working](/blog/migraine-medication-not-working).

## Conclusion: the number is a tool, not a verdict

The 15-day line separates chronic from episodic migraine on paper, but your experience doesn't switch on at 15. It's a convention that helps doctors and researchers, and even experts are debating whether it should sit lower.

What you can do is simple. Keep track of your headache days for a few months, note how often you use acute medicine, and bring that record to a doctor. Whether you're at 4 days, 12 or 20, the aim is the same: understand your pattern so you can get the right support, and, where possible, bring the number down.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many migraines a month is considered chronic?

Chronic migraine means headache on 15 or more days a month for more than three months, with migraine features on at least 8 of those days, according to the [ICHD-3 classification](https://ichd-3.org/1-migraine/1-3-chronic-migraine/). That count includes milder headache days, not just full migraine attacks.

### Can episodic migraine turn into chronic migraine?

Yes, but it doesn't happen to most people. The American Migraine Foundation estimates that [about 3% of people with episodic migraine progress each year](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/understanding-chronic-migraine/). Risk factors include frequent attacks, frequent use of acute medicine and some other health conditions, which is why a doctor can help you spot warning signs early.

### Can chronic migraine go back to episodic?

Yes. In a US study, [about 26% of people with chronic migraine remitted to episodic migraine within two years](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21270413/), and the Cleveland Clinic reports figures [from 26% to 70%](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/9638-chronic-migraine). Some people also move back and forth between the two.

### Is 12 migraine days a month chronic?

Not by the official definition, which starts at 15 headache days a month. But the American Migraine Foundation notes that [people with 8 to 14 headache days a month can be as burdened as those with 15 or more](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/defining-chronic-migraine/), so it's still worth talking to a doctor about support.

### Do I need a different doctor for chronic migraine?

Often your GP or primary care doctor is the first step, and they may refer you to a neurologist or headache specialist if needed. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/) recommends seeing a GP if attacks happen more than once a week or are hard to control.
