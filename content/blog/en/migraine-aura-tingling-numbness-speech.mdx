You are sitting at your desk when your fingertips start to buzz. Ten minutes later the tingling has crept up your arm and reached your lips, and when a colleague asks you something, the word you want simply isn't there. No zigzag lights, no blind spot, so is this migraine aura tingling, or something else?

It can be. **Migraine aura tingling** (and numbness, and trouble finding words) is a well-recognised form of aura that has nothing to do with vision. This article explains what these auras feel like, how they usually unfold, how to tell them apart from something urgent, and what to write down for your doctor. If you want the bigger picture first, our [migraine symptoms guide](/blog/migraine-symptoms) is a good starting point.

**In short**

- Aura is not always visual: it can also be tingling or numbness (sensory aura) or trouble speaking and finding words (language aura), according to [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22131-migraine-aura).
- A typical aura builds up gradually and usually fades in under an hour, while sudden symptoms or ones that last longer deserve urgent medical attention.
- Weakness on one side, double vision or poor balance are not typical aura symptoms and should be checked urgently, as set out in [NHS Scotland guidance](https://www.rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/dgrefhelp-nhs-dumfries-galloway/neurology/migraine-in-adults/).
- If a stroke is possible, don't try to work it out yourself: call emergency services.

## Why isn't aura always visual?

Aura is a group of temporary nerve symptoms that can affect more than your eyes. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22131-migraine-aura) describes visual aura (flashing lights, zigzags), sensorimotor aura (tingling, numbness or weakness in the limbs) and a less common dysphasic aura, which affects speech and language, such as mumbling or slurred speech.

Visual aura is by far the most common. The same source says aura happens in about 25 to 30% of people with migraine, and about 90% of those have a visual aura. That means sensory and language auras are less frequent, which is partly why so many people don't realise they can be migraine.

The underlying reason is that aura reflects a temporary wave of altered activity in the brain, and it can show up wherever that wave travels. If it passes through the area handling touch, you feel tingling. If it passes through areas involved in language, words get tangled. For more on what's going on in the brain, see [what causes migraines](/blog/what-causes-migraines).

If your aura is mostly visual, our guides on [flashing lights in your eyes](/blog/flashing-lights-in-eyes-migraine-aura) and [migraine with aura](/blog/migraine-with-aura) go deeper.

## What does sensory aura (tingling and numbness) feel like?

A sensory aura usually feels like pins and needles or numbness, often on one side of the body. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/) lists numbness or a tingling that feels like pins and needles among the warning signs some people get before a migraine.

[Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-with-aura/symptoms-causes/syc-20352072) gives a more detailed picture: tingling may begin in one hand and move slowly up the arm to the face, lips and tongue, and it may turn into numbness. It can also affect one side of the face.

Here are some ways women often describe it:

- A fizzing or "fizzy water" feeling in the fingers
- A hand that feels "asleep" for no reason
- A numb patch around the mouth or on the tongue
- A cheek that feels heavy or strange, as if you'd been at the dentist

The key word is **slowly**. A sensory aura tends to creep and spread rather than hit all at once, and it often affects one side of the body at a time.

## How does a sensory aura unfold, step by step?

A typical aura builds up over several minutes, then fades, usually within an hour. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-with-aura/symptoms-causes/syc-20352072) notes that aura symptoms usually last less than an hour, and the headache, if there is one, tends to follow during or after.

In practice, it often looks like this:

1. **Start:** a small area (a fingertip, a corner of the mouth) feels odd.
2. **Spread:** over minutes, the feeling travels, for example from hand to forearm to face.
3. **Fade:** the sensation recedes, sometimes leaving the area feeling a little dull or tender.
4. **Headache (or not):** a migraine headache may follow, or you may get an aura with no headache at all. Our article on [silent migraine](/blog/silent-migraine-aura-without-headache) covers that.

When someone has several types of aura in one attack, they often come one after another rather than all at once. A [research editorial on subtypes of migraine with aura](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10591443/) notes that multiple symptoms usually follow one another, most often visual first, then sensory, then language, although other orders have been seen. A sensory aura can also happen on its own.

## What happens when the words won't come? Language aura

Language aura makes it hard to find or say the right words, and it is temporary. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22131-migraine-aura) describes it as verbal and language changes such as mumbling or slurred speech. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-with-aura/symptoms-causes/syc-20352072) lists speech or language difficulty among aura symptoms too, including being unable to produce the right words.

You may notice that:

- you know exactly what you want to say but can't retrieve the word
- your sentences come out jumbled, or you swap one word for another
- you speak more slowly, or your speech sounds slurred
- you understand what people say but can't answer easily

This is often the most frightening version, because it feels like your brain has let you down. It helps to know that, in migraine, it is temporary and clears once the aura passes. But because trouble speaking is also a classic stroke symptom, a first-ever episode always needs to be taken seriously (more on that below).

## Is it aura or an urgent sign?

The difference to remember is speed and pattern: aura builds gradually and fades, while stroke symptoms tend to start suddenly. The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-and-stroke/) stresses that people with aura should know the warning signs of stroke, and research on [aura-like symptoms at the onset of stroke](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9531679/) shows the two can look similar at first, which is why doctors treat sudden new symptoms with caution.

These clues point towards aura:

- symptoms that start small and **spread over several minutes**
- a pattern you have had before
- full recovery within about an hour
- other aura features such as visual changes, or a headache that follows

These clues should make you get help fast:

- symptoms that appear **suddenly**, at full strength
- weakness in an arm, leg or one side of the face
- a first-ever episode of tingling, numbness or speech trouble
- symptoms that last longer than an hour
- double vision, loss of vision in one eye, poor balance or reduced alertness

The NHS Scotland guidance linked above states that motor weakness, double vision, visual symptoms in only one eye, poor balance or a lowered level of consciousness are not typical aura symptoms and should prompt urgent assessment. It also says an aura lasting more than an hour is a red flag.

**If you think it could be a stroke, call emergency services (999 in the UK, 911 in the US).** The American Migraine Foundation's advice on [going to the emergency department](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-in-the-emergency-department/) is to go right away for any new, sudden neurological symptoms, including weakness on one side of the face or body, or if symptoms are new, feel different or last much longer than usual. For a fuller walk-through, read [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine).

A special case is hemiplegic migraine, a rare type where the aura includes one-sided weakness. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/hemiplegic-migraine) says it can initially be mistaken for a stroke, and that you should contact emergency services if you notice stroke-like symptoms. Please don't try to decide which one it is on your own.

## Should you worry about what it means for your health?

Most of the time, aura is a migraine feature, not a sign of danger, but it is worth knowing the context. The American Migraine Foundation's page on [migraine and stroke](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-and-stroke/) reports that migraine with aura is linked to a higher risk of stroke compared with migraine without aura, while the overall risk remains low. Our article [Is migraine dangerous?](/blog/is-migraine-dangerous) looks at the studies in more detail.

For young women, one practical point comes up. The American Migraine Foundation notes that [estrogen-containing birth control](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/birth-control-and-stroke-risk/) is something to discuss carefully with a doctor if you have migraine with aura, especially if you smoke or have other vascular risk factors. Please don't change anything on your own: talk to your doctor or pharmacist about what suits you. We cover this in [birth control and migraine](/blog/birth-control-and-migraine).

None of this is meant to scare you. It's a reason to mention your aura to a healthcare professional, especially if it is new or has changed.

## What should you note for your doctor?

The most useful thing you can bring is a clear description of one or two real episodes. Details are what help a doctor judge whether your symptoms fit typical aura, and a [migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) makes this much easier than memory.

Try to write down:

- **What you felt:** tingling, numbness, trouble with words, anything visual
- **Where it started** and where it spread (for example "right fingertips, then forearm, then lips")
- **How fast it built up:** gradually over minutes, or all at once
- **How long it lasted** from start to finish
- **Which side** of the body was affected, and whether it changed sides
- **What followed:** headache, nausea, or nothing
- **What was happening beforehand:** poor sleep, stress, your cycle, long screen time
- **Anything unusual:** weakness, double vision, confusion, or an episode that felt different from your usual

If an episode is new, don't wait to build a diary: seek medical advice, and in an emergency, call for help first and write later. Our guide on [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine) can help you plan the appointment.

## What can you do during an aura?

During an aura, the practical goal is to stay safe and calm until it passes. Aura is temporary, and most of these tips are about avoiding accidents.

- **Stop what you're doing** if you're driving, cooking or using stairs, and sit somewhere safe.
- **Note the time** the aura began, so you know how long it lasts. This also helps if you ever need to describe it to a professional.
- **Keep the room calm:** dimmer light and less noise often feel easier, although this won't stop an aura.
- **Tell someone nearby** if you can, especially if your speech is affected.
- **Don't push through** a first-time or unusual episode. Get checked.

If you already have a treatment plan from your doctor, follow it. Questions about what to take, and when, are for your doctor or pharmacist, not for a blog.

## Conclusion

Aura doesn't have to be visual. Tingling, numbness and trouble finding words are recognised aura symptoms, and they usually build gradually and fade within an hour. What matters most is the pattern: slow and familiar is typical for aura, while sudden, new, one-sided weakness, or lasting more than an hour means getting urgent help.

If your aura is new or has changed, tell a healthcare professional, and keep a few notes about what you felt and how long it lasted. You don't need to figure it out alone.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can migraine cause numbness on one side of the face?

Yes. Mayo Clinic describes tingling or numbness that can affect one side of the face, or start in a hand and travel to the face, lips and tongue, as part of migraine aura. If it happens suddenly, is new for you, or comes with weakness, get urgent medical help rather than assuming it is migraine.

### How long does a sensory or speech aura last?

Aura symptoms usually last less than an hour, according to Mayo Clinic. If symptoms last longer than an hour, NHS Scotland guidance treats this as a red flag that needs urgent assessment.

### Can you have aura without a headache?

Yes. Aura can occur without any headache afterwards, which is often called silent migraine. Because the symptoms can resemble other conditions, it is still worth having them checked the first time.

### Is trouble speaking during a migraine a stroke?

Not necessarily, but the two can look alike, so a first episode should be treated as urgent. The American Migraine Foundation advises calling emergency services for any stroke-like symptoms rather than trying to tell the difference yourself.

### Do I need a brain scan for aura?

Not always. Your doctor decides based on your history and examination. Our article on [MRI for migraine](/blog/mri-for-migraine) explains when imaging is and isn't usually needed.
