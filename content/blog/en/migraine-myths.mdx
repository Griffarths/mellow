You tell a friend you have a migraine and hear, "Oh, I get headaches too." A relative says it's stress, or your liver, or the chocolate. Someone else promises you'll grow out of it. These migraine myths are everywhere, and after a while they can make you doubt your own symptoms.

This article goes through 10 of the most common ones. For each, you'll see what studies back up, what is more nuanced and what is still uncertain. If you'd like the big picture first, our guide [All about migraine](/blog/all-about-migraine) is a good starting point.

**In short**

- The American Migraine Foundation describes migraine as a neurological disease, not "just a headache" and not a mental health disorder.
- Stress, tiredness, periods and some foods or drinks can trigger attacks, according to the NHS, but the liver is not part of the NHS description of how migraine works.
- Migraine with aura is linked to a higher stroke risk, but the American Migraine Foundation says the absolute risk for any one person stays low, and brain lesions seen on scans are generally not linked to thinking problems.
- Migraine can be treated but not cured, so neither "toughing it out" nor taking painkillers very often is a good plan: your doctor can help you find a balance.

## Why are there so many migraine myths?

Migraine myths stick because almost everyone has had a headache, so almost everyone thinks they understand. In reality, migraine is very common and very disabling. [Headache disorders affect roughly 40% of people worldwide](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/headache-disorders), and [migraine ranks second among the world's causes of years lived with disability, and first among young women](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7708887/). You can read more figures in [Migraine by the numbers](/blog/migraine-by-the-numbers).

Another reason: much of a migraine attack is invisible. [Tiredness, irritability, trouble concentrating and food cravings can come before any pain](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/10-ways-to-respond-to-migraine-is-just-a-headache/), and nobody sees them. The result is that many people feel dismissed: in a [Migraine Trust survey](https://migrainetrust.org/what-we-do/policy-and-campaigns/migraine-hurts-in-many-ways-beyond-the-physical-pain/), a key theme was migraine being invalidated or called "just a headache".

If a medical word trips you up along the way, the [migraine glossary](/blog/migraine-glossary) explains each term simply.

## What people say about your head and your character

These three myths are the ones that hurt most, because they suggest the problem is you. They are also the ones studies contradict most clearly.

### Myth 1: "It's just a headache"

**Verdict: false.** The American Migraine Foundation explains that [migraine is a neurological disease with functional and sometimes structural changes in the brain](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/common-myths-about-migraine/). It often comes with nausea, sensitivity to light, sound and smells, brain fog, dizziness, and sometimes visual disturbances, numbness or speech difficulties.

Attacks can also be long: [they can last for days](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/10-ways-to-respond-to-migraine-is-just-a-headache/). And you don't need an aura (the visual or sensory warning) to have a "real" migraine, since [only about 25 to 30% of people with migraine have one](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/common-myths-about-migraine/). For the full comparison, see [Migraine vs headache](/blog/migraine-vs-headache).

### Myth 2: "It's all in your head"

**Verdict: false, with a nuance.** Migraine is [not a mental health disorder](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/common-myths-about-migraine/). It is a neurological disease with its own treatments, and you can read what happens in the brain in [What causes migraines?](/blog/what-causes-migraines).

The nuance is that migraine and mental health are connected. [Migraine is linked to both depression and anxiety, and the link goes both ways](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/link-between-migraine-depression-anxiety/): living with unpredictable pain can weigh on your mood, and many people develop migraine after a period of depression or anxiety. The American Migraine Foundation describes them as [separate conditions that may share some mechanisms](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/link-between-migraine-depression-anxiety/). Being linked is not the same as being imaginary.

### Myth 3: "You're just stressed, you need to relax"

**Verdict: partly true, but too simple.** The NHS notes that migraine is [known to be triggered by periods, stress, tiredness and certain foods or drinks](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/). So stress can matter for many people.

But stress is a trigger, not the cause. The NHS says [it isn't known exactly what causes migraine, but it involves changes to nerves and blood vessels in the brain](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/). "Just relax" also ignores that many attacks arrive when you finally slow down. More on that in [Stress and migraines](/blog/stress-and-migraines).

## What people say about food and your liver

Food myths are popular because they sound easy to fix. The evidence is more mixed.

### Myth 4: "It's your liver"

**Verdict: not supported.** The NHS describes migraine as a condition involving [changes to nerves and blood vessels in the brain](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/), and its list of known triggers (periods, stress, tiredness, certain foods and drinks) does not include the liver. We also found no recommendation from the major health organisations linking migraine to a "sluggish liver".

This doesn't mean a liver problem would never matter for your health in general. If you're worried about yours, that's a question for your doctor, separate from your migraines.

### Myth 5: "It's the chocolate (or the cheese)"

**Verdict: uncertain, and often misread.** Some people do notice that certain foods are linked to their attacks. But the American Migraine Foundation points out that [a craving for chocolate can be part of the prodrome](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/the-science-behind-migraine-triggers/), the early phase before the headache. In that case the attack was already starting, and the chocolate gets blamed unfairly.

A review of the research found that [studies giving people chocolate or a placebo did not find significant differences in the attacks triggered](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7146545/). That doesn't mean food never matters for you, only that the picture is individual. Our article [Migraine and food](/blog/migraine-and-food) goes through what is known.

### Myth 6: "The right diet will cure it"

**Verdict: false.** The American Migraine Foundation is clear that [migraine can be treated, not cured](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/10-ways-to-respond-to-migraine-is-just-a-headache/). Adjusting what you eat or drink may help some people reduce attacks as part of a wider plan, but no food, supplement or detox is a guaranteed fix. Be careful with anyone who promises one.

## What people say about risks to your brain and your body

Fear is the next myth category: "migraine will damage your brain" or "it only happens to women". Here is what research says.

### Myth 7: "Migraine damages your brain"

**Verdict: mostly false, with one nuance about aura.** Brain scans of people with migraine sometimes show small spots called white matter lesions. The American Migraine Foundation reports that [these lesions are generally not associated with neurological problems or with a higher risk of cognitive decline](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-and-brain-lesions/). In one follow-up, [people with migraine had slightly more lesions after nine years but no related neurological impairment](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-and-brain-lesions/). If you wonder whether you need a scan at all, see [MRI for migraine](/blog/mri-for-migraine).

The nuance: [migraine with aura is associated with stroke and heart disease](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-stroke-and-heart-disease/). Still, the foundation stresses that [the absolute risk for any one woman with aura remains quite low](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-stroke-reducing-risk/). The risk increases when other factors are added, [such as high blood pressure, obesity and especially smoking](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-stroke-reducing-risk/), and the foundation advises younger women with aura to [discuss contraceptives containing ethinylestradiol with their doctor](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-stroke-reducing-risk/). Please don't change anything on your own: see [Birth control and migraine](/blog/birth-control-and-migraine) and [Is migraine dangerous?](/blog/is-migraine-dangerous) for the details.

### Myth 8: "Only women get migraine"

**Verdict: false.** Migraine is more common in women, but men get it too. In adults, [about 18% of women and 6% of men have migraine](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-women/). That is roughly three times more women, which matches the figure often quoted.

Hormones are thought to play a part. The NINDS notes that [hormone changes linked to the menstrual cycle or pregnancy seem to be an important trigger, which is likely why females experience migraine more than males](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/migraine). Men's migraines are real too, and are often under-recognised.

## What people say about age and medication

### Myth 9: "You'll grow out of it"

**Verdict: sometimes, but don't count on it.** Mayo Clinic says migraines [often start around puberty and usually become less frequent as you age](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20360201). The American Migraine Foundation adds that [around 40% of boys may outgrow migraine after puberty](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-myths/), though this is less clear for girls.

Menopause is not a simple switch either. [Perimenopause, when hormones fluctuate, can make attacks more frequent for some women, while many improve afterwards](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-and-menopause/). Because it varies so much, a longer history of your own attacks is more useful than any general rule. If you're a parent reading this, our guide on [migraine in children and teens](/blog/migraine-in-children-and-teens) covers that age group.

### Myth 10: "Just tough it out" (or "just take another pill")

**Verdict: neither extreme is a good plan.** Some people feel they should avoid treatment at all costs. The American Migraine Foundation warns that [under-treating attacks can make them more intense and may increase the brain's sensitivity to pain](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/common-myths-about-migraine/).

The opposite also has a downside. Taking acute headache medication too often can cause [medication overuse headache](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/2203_AMF_Medication_Overuse_V5_Digital.pdf), where the medicine itself keeps headaches going. [Research reviews describe it as headache that develops after regular overuse of acute medication](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10442656/) in people who already have a headache disorder.

What is right for you depends on your attacks, so talk to your doctor or pharmacist about timing and frequency. For background, see [Migraine medication](/blog/migraine-medication). Non-drug approaches can sit alongside medical care: [How to relieve a migraine without medication](/blog/relieve-migraine-without-medication) looks at what has some support.

## How can you answer when someone says this?

You don't owe anyone a lecture, but a short, calm answer often works. The American Migraine Foundation has a whole list of [ways to respond to "migraine is just a headache"](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/10-ways-to-respond-to-migraine-is-just-a-headache/). A few ideas in the same spirit:

- **"It's a neurological disease."** It is more than head pain, and attacks can include nausea, light sensitivity and brain fog.
- **"There's no cure, but there are treatments."** That is why you keep going to your doctor, not because you're being dramatic.
- **"Stress can be a trigger, not the cause."** This keeps the conversation fair for both of you.

With your doctor, facts beat arguments. Writing down when attacks start, how long they last and what came before them gives you something concrete to show. Our guide on [what to write in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) can help you start.

## What to remember

Most migraine myths either blame you or promise an easy fix. The research says something calmer: migraine is a real neurological condition, triggers are personal, risks are usually small, and treatment is about management, not blame.

If one of these myths has been weighing on you, you can let it go. When something doesn't match what you live with, trust your experience and bring it to a healthcare professional.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is migraine a real disease?

Yes. The American Migraine Foundation describes [migraine as a disabling neurological disease](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/common-myths-about-migraine/) that needs its own treatments, and it is not a mental health disorder. A doctor diagnoses it from your symptoms, how severe they are and how often they happen.

### Does migraine run in families?

Often, yes. Cleveland Clinic explains that [migraines tend to run in biological families](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/are-migraines-hereditary), but the American Migraine Foundation also states that [genetics is not the only factor](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-myths/). You can still have migraine without anyone else in your family having it. See [Is migraine hereditary?](/blog/is-migraine-hereditary) for more.

### Does migraine increase the risk of stroke?

Migraine with aura is linked to a higher risk of stroke and heart disease, according to the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-stroke-and-heart-disease/). The absolute risk for one person remains low, and it rises with other factors such as smoking and high blood pressure. Ask your doctor what applies to you.

### Does chocolate trigger migraine?

For some people it may, but the evidence is weak. Studies using chocolate versus a placebo [did not find significant differences](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7146545/), and a chocolate craving can also be an early sign of an attack that has already begun.
