You have had migraines for months, maybe years, and a friend asks, "Have they done a scan?" Suddenly you wonder if your doctor missed something and whether an MRI for migraine should have been on the table. Or perhaps you are the one quietly hoping for an answer on paper, a picture that explains why your head hurts so much.

Here is the short version of **MRI for migraine**: most people with typical migraine do not need one. Your doctor can recognise migraine from your story and a physical exam, and a scan is kept for situations where something looks different. This article explains when imaging is used, why a normal result is reassuring (not an oversight), and what the scan is actually like. It also fits into our wider guide to [migraine diagnosis](/blog/migraine-diagnosis).

**In short**

- Migraine is diagnosed from your symptoms, history and a neurological exam, and [an MRI scan is not needed to diagnose primary headache disorders like migraine](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/do-i-need-an-mri-for-my-migraine/).
- Doctors usually order imaging when a headache does not fit the migraine pattern, comes with worrying signs, or changes noticeably.
- A normal scan is good news: it means your doctor has found no other cause, not that your pain is imaginary.
- When imaging is needed, MRI is usually preferred over CT for evaluating a headache disorder, while CT is used mainly in urgent situations.

## Can an MRI diagnose migraine?

No, an MRI cannot diagnose migraine, because there is no picture that shows it. According to the Mayo Clinic, [migraine diagnosis is based on your medical history, your symptoms, and a physical and neurological exam](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207).

The American Migraine Foundation explains that [imaging cannot identify the cause of a primary headache disorder](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/headache-mri-and-brain-imaging/). "Primary" simply means the headache is the condition itself, not a symptom of something else. Migraine, tension-type headache and cluster headache are all primary headaches.

So when a scan is ordered, the goal is rarely to "see" your migraine. It is to check that nothing else is going on.

## Why didn't my doctor order an MRI?

Your doctor probably did not order one because your symptoms already fit migraine and nothing in your exam raised a question. That is a sign of a careful assessment, not a rushed one.

The American Migraine Foundation says that [people who meet the criteria for migraine, have a normal exam and have not had a significant change in their headache pattern do not need imaging](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/do-i-need-an-mri-for-my-migraine/). The American Headache Society's Choosing Wisely list goes the same way: [it advises against neuroimaging in patients with stable headaches that meet criteria for migraine](https://mdedge.com/content/headache-neuroimaging-and-medication-no-nos-highlighted-new-list). The reasoning is that structural problems behind a headache are very rare, and a scan with no clear reason can lead to extra worry and extra tests.

If you are still unsure, it is fair to ask your doctor, "What made you confident this is migraine?" Our guide on [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine) has tips for preparing that conversation.

## When does a doctor order an MRI or CT scan?

A doctor orders imaging when the headache does not match the usual migraine picture or when something in your history or exam suggests another cause. The American Migraine Foundation says your doctor [might recommend an MRI if you have headache "red flags"](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/do-i-need-an-mri-for-my-migraine/), such as a sudden change in your headache pattern.

Situations where imaging is considered include:

- **A headache that does not fit the migraine pattern**, or has features your doctor finds worrying.
- **A significant change in your pattern.** The American Migraine Foundation gives the example of someone who has had occasional headaches for years and [suddenly develops a new or different type that occurs much more often](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/do-i-need-an-mri-for-my-migraine/).
- **Neurological signs on exam**, for example weakness, numbness or trouble speaking that points to a specific area of the brain.
- **A sudden, explosive headache.** This is an emergency, not a routine scan. Our article on [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) lists the warning signs, and the NHS says to [call 999 or go to A&E if a headache starts suddenly and is extremely painful](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/headaches/).
- **Aura that is unusual or comes without a headache.** The Mayo Clinic says that if your aura is not followed by head pain, your healthcare professional [might recommend tests to rule out more serious conditions such as a transient ischemic attack (TIA)](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-with-aura/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20352077). If you are not sure what counts as typical, see our guide to [migraine with aura](/blog/migraine-with-aura) and our article on [silent migraine](/blog/silent-migraine-aura-without-headache).

Doctors often use a checklist of red flags called **SNOOP**: Systemic symptoms (such as fever), a history of Neoplasm (cancer), Neurologic deficits, sudden Onset, and Older age (a first new headache after 50). Having one of these does not mean something is wrong. It means your doctor will look more closely.

One more point: a red flag is about a change, not about how bad your migraines feel. Severe, disabling attacks that follow your usual pattern are still typical migraine. For more context on safety, see [Is migraine dangerous?](/blog/is-migraine-dangerous)

## Why is a normal scan good news?

A normal scan is good news because it means no tumour, bleed, stroke or other structural problem was found, and your diagnosis stays with migraine. It does not mean the pain is "in your head" in the dismissive sense. Migraine is a real neurological condition whose changes happen in how the brain works, which a standard scan is not built to show.

If you want to understand what is happening in your brain during an attack, see [what causes migraines](/blog/what-causes-migraines).

The Mayo Clinic describes MRI as helping to find [tumours, strokes, bleeding in the brain, infections and other brain and nervous system conditions](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/brain-mri/about/pac-20582237). When none of these shows up, that is what your doctor was hoping to see.

### What if the report mentions "white matter lesions"?

Small spots called white matter lesions are sometimes seen on the MRI of people with migraine, and they are usually not a cause for alarm. A systematic review reports that [people with migraine have an increased rate of white matter lesions, but their clinical significance is uncertain](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12721971/).

Researchers are still studying what they mean, so honesty matters here: it is not fully settled. If your report mentions them, ask your doctor to explain what they think it means for you specifically. Please do not rely on a search engine to interpret your own report.

## MRI or CT: what is the difference?

An MRI uses a strong magnetic field and radio waves to make detailed pictures, while a CT scan uses X-rays. The Mayo Clinic notes that [a CT scan uses a series of X-rays to create cross-sectional images of the brain](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207).

The American Migraine Foundation explains that [CT is used in urgent situations such as strokes, brain tumours or bleeding](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/do-i-need-an-mri-for-my-migraine/). When a scan is ordered to evaluate a headache disorder, doctors generally prefer MRI, because [it is more sensitive than CT and does not involve radiation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/do-i-need-an-mri-for-my-migraine/).

In practice, if you arrive in an emergency department with a sudden severe headache, you will probably get a CT because it is fast. If your doctor is investigating a headache that is not clearly migraine, you are more likely to be offered an MRI, sometimes with contrast dye.

## What is a brain MRI like?

A brain MRI is painless but loud, and you need to lie still. The NHS says you will [lie flat on a bed, be given earplugs or headphones, and the scanner makes loud bangs and clicks](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/mri-scan/) as it takes pictures. The radiographer controls the scanner from another room, and you can hear and speak to each other during the scan.

Some practical details from the NHS:

- **Time:** the scan [takes around 15 minutes to an hour](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/mri-scan/).
- **Contrast dye:** you may be given an injection to show more detail. If so, [you may be asked to wait in hospital for up to 30 minutes](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/mri-scan/) afterwards to check you do not react to it.
- **Afterwards:** you can eat and drink as normal. Results usually take [one to two weeks](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/mri-scan/).
- **Metal in your body:** [it does not automatically rule out an MRI](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/mri-scan/who-can-have-it/), but tell the staff about any implants, pacemakers or other devices beforehand.
- **Anxiety or fear of small spaces:** the NHS advises that you [talk to a healthcare professional before your appointment](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/mri-scan/). The team can often help.

Timings and procedures can differ between hospitals and countries, so follow the instructions you are given with your appointment.

## What is a CT scan of the head like?

A CT scan is quicker. NHS hospital leaflets describe a scanner [like a large doughnut, with an X-ray machine that rotates around you](https://mft.nhs.uk/app/uploads/2023/02/Patient-information-Leaflet_CT-Scan-Information_DIV.pdf). You lie still, the radiographer leaves the room but can talk to you through an intercom, and the procedure is painless.

The scanning itself is often under a minute, though you may be in the department longer for preparation or a contrast injection. CT does use ionising radiation, which is one reason doctors prefer MRI when there is no urgency.

## How to prepare for the conversation with your doctor

Bring specifics, not just "I get bad headaches." A few weeks of notes can show whether your pattern is stable or changing, which is exactly what your doctor needs to decide about imaging. Our guide on [what to write in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) shows what to include.

Useful things to bring or ask:

- How often attacks happen, how long they last and how intense they are, and whether that has changed recently.
- Any new symptoms: different aura, weakness, numbness, vision changes, or a headache that feels unlike your usual ones.
- Whether you have had a scan before. The American Migraine Foundation says people who already had a normal MRI [do not need another if their symptoms are stable and there are no new red flags](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/do-i-need-an-mri-for-my-migraine/).
- Questions such as "What would make you order a scan?" and "What should I do if my pattern changes?"

If your vision symptoms worry you, our guides to [ophthalmic migraine](/blog/ophthalmic-migraine) and [flashing lights in the eyes](/blog/flashing-lights-in-eyes-migraine-aura) may help you describe them clearly.

## In short: imaging is the exception, not the rule

If your headaches follow a stable, typical migraine pattern and your exam is normal, not having an MRI is the standard of care, not neglect. If something changes or feels different, that is the moment to go back to your doctor, and imaging may then make sense. A normal scan is a relief, because it closes the door on the rare causes and leaves you with a condition that has real, well-studied treatments.

This article is general information, not personal medical advice. If you have a sudden severe headache or new neurological symptoms, seek urgent care, and for anything else, talk to your doctor.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a brain MRI show a migraine?

Not in a way that confirms the diagnosis. The American Migraine Foundation says imaging cannot identify the cause of primary headache disorders like migraine. A scan is used to check for other conditions, not to prove that you have migraine.

### Is an MRI better than a CT scan for headaches?

For evaluating a headache disorder, doctors generally prefer MRI. The American Migraine Foundation says MRI is more sensitive than CT and involves no radiation, while CT is used mostly in urgent situations like suspected bleeding or stroke.

### Do I need another MRI if my migraines get worse?

It depends on how they change. A gradual increase is worth discussing with your doctor, and a sudden, dramatic change or new neurological symptoms needs prompt attention. The American Migraine Foundation says a previous normal MRI does not need repeating when symptoms are stable and there are no new red flags.

### Does a normal MRI mean my migraines are not real?

No. A normal MRI means no other structural cause was found, which is reassuring. Migraine is a real neurological condition that is diagnosed from symptoms and history, which is why the Mayo Clinic describes no specific test for it.

### Can an MRI cause a migraine or is it unsafe?

An MRI uses no radiation, and the NHS describes it as painless. The loud noises and lying still can be uncomfortable, though, so tell the team if you are anxious or prone to attacks triggered by noise, and they can help you plan.
