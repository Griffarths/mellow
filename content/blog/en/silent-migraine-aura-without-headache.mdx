You're at your desk when a shimmering blind spot appears in the middle of the screen. Or your fingers start to tingle, the feeling creeps up to your lips, and the words you want just won't come out right. You wait for the pounding headache that usually follows. It never arrives, and that can be a **silent migraine**.

That's what people call a **silent migraine**: an aura without a headache. A silent migraine can be unsettling, especially the first time, but it is a recognised form of migraine, and it is often not dangerous. Here is what a silent migraine looks like, why it happens, and which signs mean you should get help quickly. If you want the bigger picture first, our [migraine symptoms guide](/blog/migraine-symptoms) is a good starting point.

**In short**

- A silent migraine (doctors say "migraine aura without headache") is an aura, such as visual changes, tingling or speech trouble, with no head pain afterwards.
- Aura symptoms usually build over a few minutes and fade within about an hour, according to the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/symptoms/).
- Only about 4% of people with migraine have aura without headache, per the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-aura-without-headache/), and it can look like a stroke or a seizure, so a first episode deserves a medical check.
- Sudden weakness on one side, a drooping face, slurred speech, confusion or a seizure are emergencies: call 999 (UK) or 911 (US).

## What is a silent migraine?

A silent migraine is a migraine aura that is not followed by a headache. The official medical term is **migraine aura without headache**, and in the past doctors called it acephalgic migraine, according to the [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/silent-migraine).

An aura is a short burst of sensory or nervous system symptoms, most often visual. Normally it comes before or alongside the pain of a migraine attack. In a silent migraine, the aura is the whole show. If you'd like to see how it fits with the other forms, our [map of the main types of migraine](/blog/types-of-migraine) lays it out simply.

The word "silent" is a bit misleading. The American Migraine Foundation points out that it [may send the wrong message](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/silent-migraine/) and downplay what you are going through. An episode can feel loud, strange and frightening even when nothing hurts.

### How common is it?

It is not very common. The American Migraine Foundation says only about 4% of people with migraine have [aura without headache](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-aura-without-headache/). It tends to appear in two age groups: young adults in their 20s and 30s, and older adults between 40 and 60.

Some people have it alongside regular migraines with headache. Others only ever get the aura. Both are possible.

## What does an aura without a headache look and feel like?

Most often, a silent migraine looks like a visual disturbance that builds over a few minutes. The Cleveland Clinic describes [bright lights, wavy or jagged lines, flashing dots or sparkles](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/silent-migraine) that seem to start in the centre of your line of sight and spread to both sides. You may also get blind spots, called scotomas.

The [NHS lists the typical aura symptoms](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/symptoms/) as:

- **Vision changes:** zigzag lines or flashing lights
- **Numbness or tingling:** pins and needles
- **Dizziness**
- **Difficulty speaking**

Tingling often follows a pattern. The Cleveland Clinic notes it can [spread from your hands to your face](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/silent-migraine). Speech trouble can feel like searching for a word you know perfectly well, or talking and hearing yourself say something muddled.

If you have also had migraines with headache, you may recognise the same aura. Our guide to [migraine with aura and its warning signs](/blog/migraine-with-aura) goes into more detail. And if what you notice seems to affect just one eye, our [ophthalmic migraine guide](/blog/ophthalmic-migraine) explains that overlap.

## How long does a silent migraine last?

Aura symptoms usually last between a few minutes and an hour. The NHS says they develop over about 5 minutes and [last for up to an hour](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/symptoms/). The Cleveland Clinic gives the same range for silent migraine: symptoms [come on within minutes and may last up to an hour](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/silent-migraine).

After the aura fades, some people feel tired, foggy or a bit "off" for a while, even without any pain. That doesn't mean something new is happening. For how the whole attack unfolds from start to finish, see [how long a migraine lasts](/blog/how-long-does-a-migraine-last).

The one-hour mark matters. The NHS advises asking for an urgent GP appointment or contacting NHS 111 if [aura symptoms last longer than an hour](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/symptoms/). In the US, calling your doctor or an urgent care line the same day is a sensible equivalent.

## Why does aura happen without pain?

Researchers know a lot about what causes aura, but not exactly why a headache sometimes doesn't follow. The leading explanation is a wave of brain activity called **cortical spreading depression**: a slow ripple of electrical change that travels across the brain's outer layer, which is considered the [neurophysiological basis of migraine aura](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8293461/). When the wave moves through the part of the brain that handles vision, you see the shimmer. When it crosses the area linked to touch, you feel tingling.

In many people, that wave can also activate pain pathways, which is thought to be why a headache often comes next. A scientific review on [aura and head pain](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6734357/) describes this link and the gaps that remain. In a silent migraine, the aura happens but the pain stage doesn't kick in. Why that is, and why it varies from person to person, is still not fully understood.

For a broader look at what is going on in the brain during an attack, see [what causes migraines](/blog/what-causes-migraines).

### Can triggers set it off?

The same triggers that bring on regular migraines may be involved, such as stress, poor sleep or hormonal shifts, but the evidence specific to silent migraine is thin. The honest answer is that this is an area where research is limited. Tracking your own episodes is the best way to see whether a pattern shows up for you, and our guide to [identifying your migraine triggers](/blog/identify-migraine-triggers) shows how.

## Is a silent migraine dangerous?

For most people, a silent migraine is not dangerous in itself. The American Migraine Foundation says that when it happens only occasionally, the impact is [usually less severe than a migraine with headache](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/silent-migraine/). The real concern is different: making sure the symptoms really are migraine aura and not something else.

Several conditions can imitate aura, particularly a transient ischaemic attack (TIA, sometimes called a "mini-stroke") and seizures. Doctors writing in a journal review on [late-onset aura](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3311824/) note that other causes, especially TIA, should be ruled out when aura appears for the first time after 40, when symptoms are only "negative" (loss of vision, for example), or when the aura is very long or very short. Without a headache to help point to migraine, the diagnosis is harder and may need tests.

That is why a first episode is worth a conversation with a doctor, even if it passed in 20 minutes and you feel completely fine afterwards.

### What about stroke risk?

There is a link between migraine with aura and stroke, but the absolute risk is small. The NHS says people with migraine, particularly with aura, have [about twice the risk of an ischaemic stroke](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/complications/) compared with people without migraine, though the risk is still small. The NHS adds that doctors generally advise women who have migraine with aura not to use the combined contraceptive pill.

This is not a reason to panic, and it is not advice for you personally. If you have aura and take hormonal contraception, or are thinking of starting it, raise it with your doctor. Our article on [birth control and migraine](/blog/birth-control-and-migraine) explains what the studies say.

## Which signs mean you should get help quickly?

Get emergency help right away if you have sudden weakness, a drooping face, slurred speech, confusion or a seizure. The NHS says to call 999 if you have [problems speaking or remembering things, lose your vision or have blurred or double vision, feel drowsy or confused, have a seizure, or have weakness on one side of your body or face](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/). In the US, call 911.

Because some aura symptoms (like speech trouble or vision changes) overlap with those warning signs, here are pointers that should push you toward help rather than waiting:

- **Weakness or numbness that makes it hard to move** an arm, leg or one side of your face. Tingling alone can be aura, but real weakness is a red flag.
- **Symptoms you have never had before**, especially if you are over 40 or pregnant.
- **Aura lasting more than an hour**, or not fully going away.
- **Confusion, drowsiness, fainting or a seizure.**
- **A sudden, extremely painful headache**, which the NHS also lists as a reason to call 999.

If you are unsure whether it's aura or something more serious, treat it as serious and call. Nobody will blame you for being cautious. Our guide on [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) walks through this in more detail.

## How to prepare before talking to your doctor

A doctor can't diagnose a silent migraine from a description alone, and often will want to rule out other causes first. The more precisely you can describe your episodes, the easier that conversation becomes. Doctors generally ask about what you saw or felt, how it spread, how long it lasted and what happened afterwards.

Try to note down:

- **What happened:** shapes, colours, which part of your vision, where the tingling started and where it went
- **How long it lasted**, with start and end times if you can
- **What you were doing** and anything unusual that day: poor sleep, stress, skipped meals, your cycle
- **Whether a headache, nausea or light sensitivity followed**, or nothing at all
- **Your family history** of migraine or stroke, and any medicines you take, including hormonal contraception

Writing it down right after an episode is far more reliable than trying to remember it a month later. Our guide to [what to write in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) shows what is actually useful.

## Key takeaways

A silent migraine is an aura without a headache, and for most people it is a short, harmless episode that fades within an hour. It is still worth getting checked the first time, because a few other conditions can look similar and only a doctor can tell them apart.

Know the red flags: weakness on one side, a drooping face, slurred speech, confusion, a seizure, or an aura that lasts longer than an hour. Those mean urgent help. For everything else, keep a simple record of your episodes and take it to your appointment. Treatment questions, including whether anything is needed at all, are for your doctor to answer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you have a migraine without a headache?

Yes. Doctors call it migraine aura without headache, or silent migraine. You get the aura symptoms, such as flashing lights or tingling, but no head pain follows, and the [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/silent-migraine) describes it as a recognised type of migraine.

### Is silent migraine the same as ocular migraine?

Not exactly. Silent migraine means an aura with no headache and can involve vision, tingling or speech. "Ocular migraine" is a loose term, usually about visual symptoms, and can refer to different things, so our [ophthalmic migraine guide](/blog/ophthalmic-migraine) explains the differences.

### Can silent migraines happen every day?

Frequent episodes are possible but worth discussing with a doctor, especially if they are new or changing. The American Migraine Foundation notes that silent migraine occurring [twice a year or less](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/silent-migraine/) tends to have a milder impact, so anything more frequent deserves a proper evaluation.

### Do I need a brain scan for a silent migraine?

Not always, and that is a decision for your doctor. Because aura without headache can look like a TIA or a seizure, doctors sometimes run tests to rule those out, particularly for a [first episode after age 40 or an unusual pattern](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3311824/).

### When should I call 999 or 911 for aura symptoms?

Call right away for weakness on one side of the body or face, trouble speaking, sudden loss of vision, confusion or a seizure, as listed by the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/). If your aura lasts more than an hour, ask for urgent medical advice the same day.
