You're at your desk when a shimmering zigzag creeps into the corner of your vision. Or your fingers start to tingle and you know exactly what's coming. The first question about migraine with aura treatment is usually the same: should I take something now, or wait?

The honest answer is that **migraine with aura treatment** mostly targets the headache, not the aura. Doctors usually suggest planning the timing of your attack medication in advance, and getting through the aura itself with rest and calm. Below, you'll find what is generally recommended for migraine with aura, what treatment can't do, and what to ask your doctor. It's general information, not personal medical advice.

**In short**

- Most attack medications, including triptans and anti-inflammatory painkillers, [don't work well when taken during the aura itself](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2026/0200/practice-guidelines-acute-migraine-treatment). They are usually aimed at the headache phase.
- Treatment doesn't shorten the aura. Most auras fade by themselves, [usually within an hour](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/symptoms/).
- If you have migraine with aura, doctors generally advise against the combined contraceptive pill because of a higher stroke risk. Other options exist, so talk to your doctor.
- Sudden weakness, slurred speech, or an aura that feels completely different from usual needs urgent medical help, not a wait-and-see approach.

## What is the treatment for migraine with aura?

Treatment for migraine with aura is the same toolkit as for other migraines: attack medication for each crisis, and sometimes preventive treatment if attacks are frequent. The aura doesn't need its own separate drug.

For attacks, [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-with-aura/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20352077) lists options such as combination painkillers, and newer oral or nasal CGRP-blocking medicines (the "gepants", such as ubrogepant, rimegepant and zavegepant) approved for migraine with or without aura. Triptans are another major class. For a full overview, see our guide to [migraine treatments](/blog/migraine-treatments), our explainer on [migraine medication](/blog/migraine-medication) and our detailed page on [triptans](/blog/triptans-explained).

For people with frequent attacks, doctors may also discuss prevention. Mayo Clinic notes that [verapamil, a blood pressure medicine, may be used to prevent migraine with aura](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-with-aura/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20352077) when other medicines haven't worked. Whether any of this suits you is a decision for your doctor, who knows your history.

## Should you take your medication during the aura or when the headache starts?

For most people, the answer from guidelines is to treat early in the headache phase, not during the aura. The [International Headache Society guidance summarised by the American Academy of Family Physicians](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2026/0200/practice-guidelines-acute-migraine-treatment) says that abortive medicines should be taken early in the headache, while pain is still mild. It also says most of them, including triptans and NSAIDs, aren't effective when taken during the aura.

This has been tested directly:

- In a trial of [subcutaneous sumatriptan given during aura](https://research.regionh.dk/en/publications/subcutaneous-sumatriptan-during-the-migraine-aura-sumatriptan-aur/), a similar share of people developed a moderate or severe headache with the drug (68%) and with placebo (75%).
- In a trial of [eletriptan taken during the aura phase](https://research.regionh.dk/da/publications/no-effect-of-eletriptan-administration-during-the-aura-phase-of-m/), the drug didn't reduce the headache either.

The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/oral-and-intranasal-triptans-for-migraine/) also says triptans are unlikely to help if taken during an aura. It adds that anti-inflammatory painkillers and anti-sickness medicines may be an option while you wait for the headache. Whether that fits you is something to agree on with your doctor.

### Why the timing is not always clear-cut

Aura and headache can overlap more than we think. In a prospective study of people with migraine with aura, [54% had pain that met migraine criteria within the first 15 minutes of the aura](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3511920). So "wait for the headache" doesn't always mean a long wait.

Sources don't fully agree here. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22131-migraine-aura) suggests taking a doctor-approved medicine within the first 15 minutes of aura symptoms. The trial evidence above points the other way for triptans. This is exactly why it's worth asking your doctor for a clear plan, instead of guessing in the moment.

## What treatment doesn't change: the aura itself

Attack medication is designed to calm the headache and its companions like nausea. It isn't known to shorten or switch off the aura. The aura is a temporary wave of altered brain activity, and it usually runs its course on its own.

[NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/symptoms/) describes aura as symptoms such as flashing lights, zigzag patterns, blind spots, tingling or numbness, dizziness and difficulty speaking. They typically build over about five minutes and last up to an hour. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22131-migraine-aura) gives a range of five to 60 minutes.

If you've been disappointed that your medication "didn't stop the aura", it may help to know that this is expected. For non-visual symptoms, see [migraine aura with tingling, numbness and speech trouble](/blog/migraine-aura-tingling-numbness-speech). For visual ones, see [flashing lights in your eyes](/blog/flashing-lights-in-eyes-migraine-aura).

## What can you do during the aura?

Because the aura passes by itself, the most useful steps are about safety and comfort. None of these cure the aura, but they make it easier to get through.

- **Stop and sit down.** Pause driving, cycling, cooking or using stairs until your vision or balance is back to normal. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22131-migraine-aura) advises stopping what you're doing when you notice aura.
- **Find a quiet, dim place.** [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-with-aura/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20352077) suggests heading to a quiet, darkened room, closing your eyes and resting when symptoms start.
- **Note the time it starts.** This helps you track how long your aura lasts and when the headache follows.
- **Have your plan ready.** If your doctor has agreed on a plan, keep your medication, water and a snack within reach, so you aren't searching for them with blurry vision.
- **Tell someone.** A message to a colleague, friend or flatmate means someone knows why you've gone quiet.

Staying calm helps too. Aura can be frightening, especially the first times, but a typical aura that fades within an hour and matches your usual pattern is [not a sign of danger in itself](/blog/migraine-with-aura). If light and noise are hard to bear, our page on [light sensitivity in migraine](/blog/migraine-light-sensitivity) has practical ideas.

## Birth control and migraine with aura: what precautions apply?

If you have migraine with aura, doctors generally advise against combined hormonal contraception, meaning methods that contain oestrogen. The reason is stroke risk, which is small in absolute terms but real.

[NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/complications/) says that people with migraine with aura have around twice the risk of ischaemic stroke compared with people without migraine, and that the combined pill adds to that risk. The [NHS page on who can take the combined pill](https://www.nhs.uk/contraception/methods-of-contraception/combined-pill/who-can-take-it/) says that if you take it and start getting aura symptoms, you should talk to a GP about other contraception. A [consensus statement from European headache and contraception experts](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5662520/) takes the same careful approach.

A few points worth knowing:

- Migraine **without** aura is treated differently. The NHS says people with it can usually take the combined pill unless they have other stroke risk factors, such as high blood pressure.
- If you start getting aura after beginning a combined pill, or your aura changes, tell your GP or doctor soon rather than waiting for the next review.
- Never stop a contraceptive on your own without a backup plan. Ask your doctor about alternatives that don't contain oestrogen.

Our full guide, [birth control and migraine](/blog/birth-control-and-migraine), goes through what the studies say.

## Are there special cases for triptans and aura?

Yes. Triptans narrow blood vessels, so they aren't suitable for everyone. The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/oral-and-intranasal-triptans-for-migraine/) lists reasons doctors may avoid them, including heart disease, a history of stroke, uncontrolled high blood pressure, and very long, severe or hemiplegic auras (aura with weakness on one side of the body).

The same organisation explains that triptans are officially contraindicated in migraine with brainstem aura, because people with it were excluded from the original trials. That doesn't mean they'll always harm you, but it does mean the decision belongs with a specialist.

If your aura includes weakness, trouble speaking, double vision or loss of balance, tell your doctor at your next appointment, even if it passes. They'll want to choose your medication with that in mind.

## When does an aura mean you shouldn't wait it out?

An aura that is new, different or involves weakness should be treated as an emergency until a professional says otherwise. Most auras are harmless, but a few signs deserve a call straight away.

The [NHS says to call 999](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/symptoms/) if you have:

- weakness or paralysis in one or both arms, or one side of the face
- slurred or garbled speech
- a sudden, agonising headache unlike anything you've had before
- headache with a high temperature, stiff neck, confusion, seizures, double vision or a rash

In the US, call 911. Don't try to decide on the spot whether it's "just your aura". Our guide on [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) explains the thresholds in more detail.

## What if your treatment doesn't seem to work?

Before deciding a medicine has failed, check the timing and the context. Medication taken too early (during aura) or too late (when the pain is already severe), or when you're vomiting, can look like it's "not working".

Our article on [migraine medication not working](/blog/migraine-medication-not-working) covers timing, nausea and other common reasons. Keep a short record of each attack: when the aura started, when you took your medication, and how you felt two hours later. If you use attack medication very often, [medication overuse headache](/blog/medication-overuse-headache) is something to ask about.

## How to prepare for your doctor's appointment

A clear picture of your aura makes treatment decisions much easier. Our guide on [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine) explains who to book and what to bring. Useful things to note:

- what your aura looks and feels like, and how long it lasts
- how long after the aura the headache starts
- which medicines you've tried, when you took them, and what happened
- any contraception you use, and any family history of stroke or heart disease
- anything that was unusual about a particular aura

## The takeaway

For most people, migraine with aura treatment comes down to three habits: protect yourself while the aura lasts, treat the headache early according to your doctor's plan, and review contraception if you use hormones. The aura will usually pass by itself, and that's normal.

If your pattern changes, or you're unsure about timing, ask your doctor to write out a plan that fits your attacks. Bring a few weeks of notes, and it will be much easier.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you stop a migraine aura once it has started?

There's no proven way to stop an aura once it begins. Attack medication is mainly aimed at the headache, and most auras fade by themselves in under an hour. Resting in a calm, dim place and waiting it out is generally the safest approach.

### Should I take a triptan as soon as I see the aura?

Trials of sumatriptan and eletriptan taken during aura found they didn't prevent the headache, and the American Migraine Foundation says triptans are unlikely to work in that phase. Many doctors suggest taking them when the headache starts, but your own plan should come from your doctor.

### Can I take the combined pill if I have migraine with aura?

The NHS advises people with migraine with aura generally not to use the combined pill because it increases stroke risk. If you already take it and notice aura, talk to your GP or doctor about other contraception rather than stopping without a backup.

### How long does a migraine aura last?

Usually between five and 60 minutes, according to Cleveland Clinic, and up to about an hour according to the NHS. An aura lasting much longer than usual, or with weakness or slurred speech, should be checked urgently.
