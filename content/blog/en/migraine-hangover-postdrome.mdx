The pain finally fades, and you expect to feel like yourself again. Instead, you're drained, foggy, a little achy, and reading the same sentence three times. If that sounds familiar, you're probably dealing with a **migraine hangover**, the recovery phase doctors call the postdrome.

It's a real part of the attack, not laziness and not "in your head". In this article we'll look at what the migraine hangover is, how common it is, how long it usually lasts, and how you can spot it in your diary so you can plan the day after. It's one of several phases we cover in our guide to [migraine symptoms](/blog/migraine-symptoms).

**In short**

- The migraine hangover (postdrome) is the final phase of an attack, when the headache is gone but you can still feel tired, foggy and off.
- It's very common: in a study of 120 people, [81% reported at least one non-headache symptom after the pain ended](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4955275).
- It usually lasts from a few hours up to about 48 hours, and in most attacks people were back to normal within 24 hours in that same study.
- Writing down when the pain ends and how you feel the next day helps you see your own pattern and plan around it.

## What is a migraine hangover (postdrome)?

A migraine hangover is the fourth and last phase of a migraine attack, the one that comes after the headache. The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-hangover/) describes postdrome as the medical term for it, and the name fits: many people say it feels like the day after a night of drinking, without the night out.

A migraine attack can have up to four phases: the early warning phase (prodrome), aura in some people, the headache itself, and then the postdrome. If you want to learn about the warning phase, we've written about [migraine prodrome symptoms](/blog/migraine-prodrome-symptoms). The key point is that your attack isn't necessarily over when the pain stops. The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/how-to-deal-with-postdrome/) notes that people in postdrome are still going through part of the attack.

## What does a migraine hangover feel like?

The most common sign is simply feeling tired and weary, followed by trouble concentrating and a stiff neck. The [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/migraine-hangover-postdrome) lists fatigue, body aches, difficulty concentrating, nausea, sensitivity to light and sound, dizziness and mood changes as typical symptoms.

In the 120-person electronic diary study published in Neurology, [being tired or weary showed up in 88% of postdromes, difficulty concentrating in 56% and a stiff neck in 42%](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4955275). A later review that pooled several studies found a similar picture: [fatigue in about half of people, concentration problems in about a third, and mood changes in just under a third](https://research.regionh.dk/en/publications/what-proportion-of-people-with-migraine-report-postdromal-symptom/). Those numbers vary a lot from study to study, so treat them as a rough guide.

Here's what your own postdrome might include:

- Heavy tiredness, like you've run a marathon
- Brain fog, slow thinking, trouble finding words
- A sore or stiff neck and general body aches
- Leftover sensitivity to light, sound or smells
- Mild nausea or low appetite
- Feeling dizzy or unsteady (if this is a big part of your attacks, our article on [vestibular migraine](/blog/vestibular-migraine-dizziness-vertigo) may help)
- Mood changes, from flat and low to unusually upbeat

Not everyone gets all of these, and not every attack has the same postdrome. Some people feel strangely relieved and energetic after the pain lifts, which is also recognised as part of the phase.

## Why are you so tired after a migraine?

Nobody knows exactly why the postdrome happens, so the honest answer is: probably a mix of things. Your brain has just been through an intense event, and many people also had a poor night, skipped meals or barely drank anything while the pain was at its worst.

Researchers are still working out what is going on in the brain during this phase. What we can say is that the symptoms are common enough to be considered part of the attack itself. That's why the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/how-to-deal-with-postdrome/) suggests treating this phase with care rather than expecting to bounce back straight away.

A few ordinary factors can add to the exhaustion, and they're worth noticing in your own life:

- **Sleep:** pain and nausea often wreck your night. Our article on [migraine and sleep](/blog/migraine-and-sleep) explains how closely the two are linked.
- **Not eating or drinking much** during the attack.
- **Medication side effects:** some people feel drowsy after taking something for the pain. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist if this worries you, and read our overview of [migraine medication](/blog/migraine-medication) for the general picture.

## How long does a migraine hangover last?

Most migraine hangovers last from a few hours up to 48 hours. That range comes from the [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/migraine-hangover-postdrome), and it fits what researchers found when they followed people day by day.

In the Neurology diary study, [93% of attacks ended with a return to normal within 24 hours after the pain resolved](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4955275). So one day is the most common experience, but a second day of feeling off is not unusual and does not mean something is wrong.

This matters when you count how much time a migraine really takes from your week. The headache might last a few hours, but with the hangover on top, the attack can take over a day or two. For the full timeline, see [how long a migraine lasts](/blog/how-long-does-a-migraine-last).

## Postdrome, prodrome or a new attack: how can you tell them apart?

The simplest way is to look at the order of events. Prodrome comes **before** the headache (yawning, cravings, neck stiffness, mood shifts), while postdrome comes **after** it, when the pain has eased. The two can feel very similar, which is why a diary helps.

Here are a few clues:

- **Postdrome:** the pain is gone or much reduced, and you feel tired, foggy or achy, usually gradually improving.
- **Prodrome:** you feel off hours or a day before any pain starts.
- **A new or returning attack:** the headache comes back or builds again, with the same features as before.

Sometimes the lines blur, especially if attacks are frequent. If your head pain keeps returning or your migraine drags on for days, read [migraine that won't go away](/blog/migraine-that-wont-go-away). And if you ever have sudden, severe or unusual symptoms, check our guide on [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine).

## How can you spot your migraine hangover in your diary?

Track the day after, not only the attack. Most diaries stop when the pain ends, so the recovery day stays invisible, and that's exactly the day you want to plan for.

For each attack, you could note:

1. **When the pain started and when it ended** (the time of day is enough).
2. **How you felt the next morning:** tired, foggy, achy, sensitive to light, low mood, or fine.
3. **When you felt back to normal:** that evening, the next day, or the day after.
4. **What helped or made it worse:** rest, a walk, screens, caffeine, skipped meals.
5. **What you had to cancel or push through** (work, classes, plans).

After a few attacks, you'll likely see a pattern. Maybe your hangover is short after a night attack but longer after one at work. Maybe the day after is always your worst concentration day. Our guide to [what to write in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) goes deeper into what's worth recording.

## How can you organise the day after an attack?

Plan for a slower day and be kind to yourself, because pushing through can backfire. According to the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/how-to-deal-with-postdrome/), ignoring or pushing through the postdrome doesn't give your body time to recover and can raise the risk of another attack.

Some ideas the American Migraine Foundation and Cleveland Clinic mention, and that many people find helpful:

- **Rest and drink water.** [Staying hydrated through all phases](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/how-to-deal-with-postdrome/) is part of their advice.
- **Avoid overstimulation.** Bright lights, strong smells and long screen sessions can keep symptoms going. Our article on [migraine and screens](/blog/migraine-and-screens) looks at what's known about this.
- **Move gently.** Getting out of bed, stretching or doing something very light may help recovery.
- **Use comfort measures.** Ice packs, heating pads, a massage, a simple comfort meal or a calming activity like yoga work for some people.
- **Be careful with alcohol.** If you're not feeling well, it's probably not the day for it. We explain why in [alcohol and migraine](/blog/alcohol-and-migraine).

On a practical level, there are small things you can do ahead of time. If you know you're likely to need a recovery day after an attack, keep your most demanding tasks flexible when you can, and let a friend, teacher or manager know you may work at half speed. Prepare an easy meal, and put off important decisions or exams if you can.

None of this is a treatment plan. If you wonder whether a medication could help your attacks or your recovery, that's a conversation for your doctor.

## When should you talk to your doctor about it?

Talk to your doctor if the hangover regularly stops you from working, studying or looking after yourself, or if it feels different from what you're used to. A migraine hangover is common, but you shouldn't have to simply put up with a lot of lost days.

Make an appointment if:

- Your recovery days add up to a big part of the month
- Your symptoms last longer than they used to
- You're not sure whether you're in postdrome or having another attack
- You feel low or anxious around your attacks

Bring your diary with you, since it gives your doctor concrete information. Our guide on [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine) explains who to book and how to prepare.

## The takeaway

The migraine hangover is a normal, well-documented part of many attacks, and feeling wrecked afterwards doesn't mean you're weak or exaggerating. Expect it to last anywhere from a few hours to about two days, with the first day usually the hardest.

The most useful thing you can do is track it. Once you know how long your own recovery takes, you can protect that time instead of being surprised by it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a migraine hangover the same as postdrome?

Yes. "Migraine hangover" is the everyday name for the postdrome, the phase after the headache. The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-hangover/) uses the terms interchangeably.

### How common is the migraine hangover?

It's very common, though not universal. In one diary study of 120 people, [81% had at least one non-headache symptom after the pain ended](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4955275), and a pooled review of clinic-based studies found [an average of 86%](https://research.regionh.dk/en/publications/what-proportion-of-people-with-migraine-report-postdromal-symptom/).

### Can you have a migraine hangover without a headache?

Possibly, since the phases are separate from the pain. If you get aura without a headache, our article on [silent migraine](/blog/silent-migraine-aura-without-headache) explains how those attacks can look. Talk to your doctor if you aren't sure what you're experiencing.

### Can a migraine hangover last more than two days?

Most are shorter, with the Cleveland Clinic giving a range of [a few hours up to 48 hours](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/migraine-hangover-postdrome). If you feel unwell for longer, or the headache keeps coming back, it's worth discussing with your doctor.

### Does everyone feel tired after a migraine?

No. Tiredness is the most common postdrome symptom, but some people mostly notice foggy thinking, a stiff neck or mood changes, and others feel very little. Your own diary will show what your pattern is.
