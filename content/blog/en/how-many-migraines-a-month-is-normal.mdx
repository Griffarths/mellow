You get a migraine, recover, and then wonder: how many migraines a month is normal, and is this just my life now? Maybe your friend has one twice a year, while you seem to lose a few days every month. Or you have lost count altogether, and you are not sure whether that is "a lot".

So, **how many migraines a month is normal**? There is no single normal number. Some people have one or two attacks a year, others have several a month, and both can be migraine. What matters more is how your own pattern looks over time and how much it disrupts your life.

This article is part of our guide to [migraine symptoms](/blog/migraine-symptoms). It will help you place your frequency without trying to diagnose you, and know when it is worth tracking and talking to a doctor.

**In short**

- There is no fixed "normal": migraine can happen once a year or once a week, and [Cleveland Clinic notes](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/5005-migraine-headaches) that many people have around two to four a month.
- Doctors call migraine "chronic" when headaches occur on [15 or more days a month for at least three months](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/9638-chronic-migraine), with migraine features on at least 8 of those days.
- Frequency is only one piece of the picture: how severe your attacks are and how much they affect your work and life count just as much.
- If your attacks come more than once a week, or you take painkillers often, [the NHS advises seeing a GP](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/), and a simple diary makes that appointment far more useful.

## Is there a "normal" number of migraines per month?

No, there is not one normal number, because migraine frequency varies a lot from person to person. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/5005-migraine-headaches) describes it this way: you might have one migraine a year or one a week, and most people with migraine fall somewhere around two to four a month.

The NHS says something similar: [some people have attacks several times a week, while for others years can pass between attacks](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/). Neither pattern is "more real" than the other.

It also changes over a lifetime. Hormones, stress, sleep, new medication or a busy season of life can make a quiet period turn into a rough one, and the reverse can happen too. So the most useful question is not "am I normal?" but "is my pattern stable, getting worse, or getting in my way?"

## Which frequency groups do doctors use?

Doctors mostly split migraine into two big groups: episodic (fewer than 15 headache days a month) and chronic (15 or more). The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/understanding-chronic-migraine/) uses exactly this line, and it is the one most guidelines are built on.

In everyday language, you can think of it as a spectrum:

- **A few attacks a year:** occasional migraine. Many people live like this for years without needing anything beyond a plan for the attack itself.
- **One to a few attacks a month:** the range many people recognise, and the one Cleveland Clinic describes as common.
- **Around 8 to 14 headache days a month:** still "episodic" on paper, but a heavy load that doctors take seriously.
- **15 or more headache days a month, for three months:** the territory of chronic migraine.

Note that these are headache days, not necessarily full attacks. A single long attack that lasts three days counts as three days. If you want to understand how long one attack can run, we cover it in [how long a migraine lasts](/blog/how-long-does-a-migraine-last).

## A few attacks a year: is it still migraine?

Yes, it can be. Having only a few attacks a year does not rule migraine out, as migraine is defined by the features of the attack (such as one-sided throbbing pain, nausea, sensitivity to light and sound), not by how often it happens.

Mayo Clinic lists these typical symptoms, including [nausea and sensitivity to light and sound](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20360201). If your rare headaches look like that, it may be migraine, but only a health professional can tell you for sure.

Rare attacks can still be hard on you. One severe attack that wipes out a day and leaves you drained afterwards is a big deal, even if it only happens twice a year. If you are curious about the different shapes migraine can take, our [simple map of the types of migraine](/blog/types-of-migraine) is a good place to start.

## Several migraines a month: should you worry?

Having several migraines a month is common and not, by itself, a sign that something dangerous is going on. It is, however, a good reason to look at your pattern more closely, and to ask whether your current plan is enough.

The NHS suggests seeing a GP if your attacks are [severe or getting worse, more than once a week, or hard to control](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/). Mayo Clinic gives a similar benchmark: see a doctor if you have [two or more headaches a week](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/chronic-daily-headaches/symptoms-causes/syc-20370891) or if your headaches disrupt your work or personal life.

If you are curious about the bigger safety picture, we looked at it in [is migraine dangerous](/blog/is-migraine-dangerous). The short version: migraine is not usually dangerous in itself, but it deserves proper care.

## Why the number of days does not tell the whole story

The number of days is a useful marker, but it does not capture how bad those days are. Two people can each have four attacks a month, and one may be able to carry on with a tablet and a dark room while the other is in bed for two days.

Here is what else is worth noticing:

- **Intensity:** mild, moderate or severe pain.
- **Duration:** a few hours, or several days.
- **Impact:** missed work, cancelled plans, days where you function at half speed.
- **Recovery:** the hangover-like tiredness after an attack, which is part of the cycle. Our article on [the migraine hangover](/blog/migraine-hangover-postdrome) explains it.
- **Response to treatment:** whether what you use actually helps, and how quickly.

This is also why the American Migraine Foundation, when it discusses preventive treatment, does not rely on a single cut-off. It says that preventive options are often considered when people have [more than four attacks a month, or attacks that are very severe](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-prevention-101/). Frequency and impact are weighed together.

## When does it become chronic migraine?

Chronic migraine means headaches on at least 15 days a month for more than three months, with migraine features on at least 8 of those days. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/9638-chronic-migraine) lays out these criteria, and Mayo Clinic describes the same 15-day line for [chronic daily headaches](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/chronic-daily-headaches/symptoms-causes/syc-20370891).

It does not mean "your migraine is permanent". Studies show that people can move between categories over time, and the line itself is a convention that researchers keep discussing. If you would like a closer look at this boundary, see [chronic vs episodic migraine and the 15-day line](/blog/chronic-migraine-vs-episodic-migraine).

If your headaches are close to daily but do not feel like migraine, a different cause may be involved, which is covered in [why you might have a headache every day](/blog/headache-every-day). A doctor can sort out which it is.

## When can a high frequency lead to a talk about prevention?

If you have a lot of attacks, a doctor may bring up preventive treatment, which is taken regularly to make attacks less frequent or less severe. The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/preventive-migraine-treatment/) says the decision weighs frequency, impact on daily life, how well other treatments work, side effects and your preferences, and that it is made together with your healthcare professional.

You will not decide this alone, and it does not have to be a big commitment. Our overview of [migraine prevention medication](/blog/migraine-prevention-medication) explains the main families so you can arrive at the appointment prepared.

One more reason frequency matters: frequent painkiller use can backfire. Mayo Clinic notes that the risk of [medication overuse headache](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/rebound-headaches/symptoms-causes/syc-20377083) goes up when certain medicines (such as triptans or combination painkillers) are used 10 or more days a month, or simple painkillers more than 15 days a month. If that sounds familiar, do not change anything on your own, and speak to your doctor. We explain the cycle in [medication overuse headache](/blog/medication-overuse-headache).

## How do you count your attacks so a doctor can use them?

The most useful thing you can bring to an appointment is a few weeks of notes, because memory is a poor counter, especially for painful days. The NHS recommends [keeping a migraine diary](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/) to spot patterns and possible triggers.

Keep it simple. For each attack, jot down:

1. The date, and roughly when it started and ended.
2. How strong the pain was (for example on a scale of 1 to 10).
3. Other symptoms: nausea, aura, light or sound sensitivity.
4. What you took and whether it helped.
5. Any possible triggers: poor sleep, a skipped meal, stress, your cycle.

Then count two numbers at the end of the month: how many days you had any headache, and how many days you took pain relief. Those two figures are exactly what a GP or neurologist will want. Our guide on [what to write down in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) goes into more detail, and [reading your calendar, duration and intensity](/blog/migraine-diary-patterns) shows how to make sense of the result.

You can also turn it into a short summary. [What to put in a migraine report for your doctor](/blog/migraine-report-for-doctor) gives you a template.

## When should you not wait to get help?

Some situations call for a prompt appointment, and a few need urgent care. Do not wait if your attacks are getting more frequent, you take painkillers most weeks, your usual pattern has changed, or migraine is affecting your work, studies or mood.

For the next step, [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine) explains who to book with and how to prepare.

Some symptoms are different: a sudden, severe "worst headache of your life", weakness, confusion, trouble speaking or a head injury need emergency care, not a diary. Our article on [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) lists the warning signs.

## What to remember

There is no magic number of migraines that makes you "normal" or "too much". A few a year, a few a month and many a month can all be migraine, and what matters is your trend, the severity and the toll on your life.

A practical plan: count your headache days and painkiller days for a month or two, note how bad the attacks feel and what they cost you, and take that to your doctor if the pattern is rising, close to weekly, or hard to cope with. You deserve care whether you have two attacks a year or twenty a month.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is having a migraine every week normal?

It is not unusual, but it is a good reason to see your doctor. The NHS advises seeing a GP if you have [migraine attacks more than once a week](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/), because there are often ways to improve the situation.

### Is 4 migraines a month a lot?

Four migraines a month is around the upper end of what Cleveland Clinic describes as the typical range of two to four. The American Migraine Foundation says that [more than four attacks a month](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-prevention-101/) is one of the points where preventive options are worth discussing, so it is worth raising with your doctor, especially if the attacks are severe.

### Can you have only one migraine a year?

Yes. Cleveland Clinic says you might have [one migraine a year or one a week](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/5005-migraine-headaches). Migraine is recognised by what the attack feels like, not by how often it comes, though a doctor is the one to confirm it.

### Can your migraine frequency change over time?

Yes, it often does. Life stages, stress, sleep, hormones and medication use can all push the number up or down, and people can move between episodic and chronic patterns in both directions. Tracking your attacks over months is the easiest way to notice a trend early.
