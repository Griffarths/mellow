You're mid-attack and the bedroom light feels like a spotlight. That's migraine light sensitivity: even a normal lamp becomes too much. Your partner's footsteps sound like furniture being dragged, and the smell of someone's coffee in the next room makes your stomach turn. Nothing has changed in the room, but everything has become too much.

This is **migraine light sensitivity** and its two close companions, sensitivity to sound and to smells. It is a real, well-studied part of the attack, not you being dramatic. Below, we explain why it happens, when it shows up, and how to notice and note it so it becomes useful information. It's one of the key features covered in our [migraine symptoms guide](/blog/migraine-symptoms).

**In short**

- Sensitivity to light (photophobia), sound (phonophobia) and smells (osmophobia) is a [common part of a migraine attack](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20360201), and it comes from how the migraine brain processes sensory signals.
- Between [85% and 90% of people with migraine](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/photophobia) report light sensitivity at least sometimes.
- The sensitivity can linger between attacks too: [about three-quarters of people with migraine report sound sensitivity between attacks](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4038337).
- Noting when and how these sensitivities appear helps you recognize your attacks and gives your doctor useful details.

## What are photophobia, phonophobia and osmophobia?

These are the medical names for sensitivity to light, sound and smells, and all three are common in migraine. The "phobia" part doesn't mean fear. It comes from a Greek word and here simply means that the stimulus is unpleasant or painful.

- **Photophobia:** light feels too bright, uncomfortable or actually painful. [Cleveland Clinic describes it](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/photophobia) as squinting, blinking a lot, shielding your eyes, or preferring dim light.
- **Phonophobia:** ordinary sounds (a TV, cutlery, a notification ping) feel too loud or sharp.
- **Osmophobia:** certain smells, like perfume, cooking, smoke or cleaning products, feel overwhelming or make you feel sick.

You can find these and other terms in our [migraine glossary](/blog/migraine-glossary). The [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-symptoms-faq/) also lists increased sensitivity to light, sound or smells among the common migraine symptoms.

## Why does your brain become so sensitive during a migraine?

During an attack, the brain areas that handle sensory information become more reactive, so normal signals are felt as too intense. Researchers don't have every piece of the puzzle, but some parts are well described, especially for light.

A well-known study from Harvard researchers looked at people who were blind and had migraine. Those with [no light perception at all, because of a non-functioning optic nerve, reported no photophobia](https://content.news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2010/01/light-worsens-migraine-headaches). Blind participants who could still detect light and dark through light-sensing cells in the retina did report that light made their headache worse.

The same work found a crossing point in the thalamus, a relay station deep in the brain. There, fibers carrying light signals from specialized retinal cells overlap with neurons that are part of the migraine pain circuit. In plain words: light and pain signals meet in the same neighborhood, which may be why light can make a migraine hurt more.

For sound and smell, the picture is less complete. A [scientific review of sensory processing in migraine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4038337) describes a pattern of multisensory hypersensitivity (touch, sight, hearing and smell) that appears to be a characteristic feature of migraine itself. Brain imaging has also shown different activation patterns in people with migraine who are sensitive to odors, compared with people without migraine.

If you'd like the bigger picture of what happens in the brain, our article on [what causes migraines](/blog/what-causes-migraines) walks through it simply.

## When do these sensitivities show up during a migraine?

Most often, they peak during the headache phase, but they can also appear before it and linger between attacks. A migraine isn't only the moment the pain starts, and your senses may tell you about it at different points.

### Before the pain

Some people notice that light, sound or smells suddenly feel sharper hours before the headache begins. This early phase is called the prodrome, and it can include other subtle changes such as tiredness or yawning. Our guide to [migraine prodrome symptoms](/blog/migraine-prodrome-symptoms) covers the early signs in more detail.

### During the headache

This is when sensitivity usually hits hardest. The [NHS notes](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/) that being very sensitive to light and sounds is a main symptom of an attack, which is why many people want to rest in a quiet, dark room. Research also shows that people with migraine are [even more sensitive to sound during an attack](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4038337) than between attacks.

### After the pain, and in between

After the headache fades, you may still feel raw and easily overwhelmed. This is part of what's known as the postdrome, which we describe in [migraine hangover (postdrome)](/blog/migraine-hangover-postdrome). Between attacks, studies show people with migraine tend to have a [lower threshold for visual discomfort](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-symptoms-faq/) than people without migraine. So if you feel more bothered by bright lights than your friends even on a "good" day, you're not imagining it.

If you're wondering how long all of this lasts, see [how long a migraine lasts](/blog/how-long-does-a-migraine-last).

## Can light, noise and smell sensitivity help tell migraine from other headaches?

Yes, these symptoms are one of the clues doctors use to recognize migraine. In the international criteria clinicians use, a migraine without aura needs, during the headache, [either nausea and/or vomiting, or both light and sound sensitivity](https://thejournalofheadacheandpain.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1129-2377-15-2/tables/1), alongside other features such as pulsating pain or worsening with movement.

The [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20360201) also notes that headache with light sensitivity, reduced ability to function and nausea suggests migraine is likely. This is a screening idea, not a diagnosis, and only a doctor can confirm it.

Smell sensitivity is interesting here. In a large study of 2,883 people, [osmophobia was reported by 62.2% of people with migraine, versus 14.5% of those with tension-type headache](https://scholar.nycu.edu.tw/en/publications/clinical-correlates-and-diagnostic-utility-of-osmophobia-in-migra/). That suggests smell sensitivity points toward migraine, though it isn't present in every attack and can't diagnose anything on its own.

To compare the two in more depth, read [migraine vs headache](/blog/migraine-vs-headache).

## Are light, noise and smells symptoms of the attack or triggers?

They can be both, and it's often hard to tell which came first. A bright light or a strong perfume might genuinely set off an attack, or it might just feel unbearable because the attack had already begun.

Some facts help here:

- About [half of people with migraine report odor-triggered attacks](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4038337).
- The American Migraine Foundation says that [strong smells like perfume, gasoline or chemical odors can trigger an attack or make one worse](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-home-remedies/).
- For light, the story with screens is more nuanced than "blue light is the villain", as we explain in [migraine and screens](/blog/migraine-and-screens).

So how do you work out which is which? This is where patterns over time matter, and our guide on [how to identify your migraine triggers](/blog/identify-migraine-triggers) can help you sort it out step by step.

## How can you spot and note your sensitivities?

The easiest way is to write down a few simple details each time, so your sensitivities become patterns instead of vague memories. You don't need a long entry. A few seconds during or just after the attack is enough.

Here are useful things to note:

1. **Which senses were affected:** light, sound, smell, or all three.
2. **When it started:** before the pain, with the pain, or only at the peak.
3. **How strong it was:** mild annoyance, hard to tolerate, or forcing you to leave a room.
4. **Specific things that bothered you:** sunlight, fluorescent office lights, a phone screen, traffic, a particular perfume or food smell.
5. **What was happening around you:** a poor night of sleep, a stressful day, a missed meal, your period.
6. **Whether anything helped,** and how long the attack lasted.

Over a few weeks, you may notice that your light sensitivity arrives a few hours before the pain, or that smells bother you only during certain weeks of your cycle. That kind of insight is hard to see without a record. Our guides on [what to write in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) and [reading migraine diary patterns](/blog/migraine-diary-patterns) show how to turn notes into something useful.

## What can you do when everything bothers you?

During an attack, the goal is simply to lower the sensory load and be gentle with yourself. These are general comfort ideas, not medical advice or treatment.

- **Find a darker, quieter space.** This is what [many people instinctively do](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/migraine/), and the American Migraine Foundation also suggests [finding a quiet place away from outside sounds](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-home-remedies/).
- **Step away from strong smells.** Open a window, leave the kitchen, or ask people nearby to skip perfume for a while.
- **Dim your screens** or take a break from them when you can.
- **Ask for help** from people around you. Saying "I'm having a migraine, lights and noise are too much" is enough.

For more ideas that don't involve pills, see [how to relieve a migraine without medication](/blog/relieve-migraine-without-medication).

### What about tinted glasses?

Some people find tinted lenses helpful in bright environments. Cleveland Clinic notes that [the American Migraine Foundation says tinted lenses may help](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/photophobia), and that a doctor may suggest lenses with a particular tint such as FL-41. Evidence is still limited and not every product sold as "migraine glasses" is the same, so it's worth asking your doctor or an eye care professional before buying.

Cleveland Clinic also explains that when light sensitivity comes from migraine, [treating the migraine usually relieves it](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/photophobia). If your attacks are frequent, talk to your doctor about treatment options. Our [migraine medication overview](/blog/migraine-medication) explains what exists, but the right choice is always something to decide with a professional.

## When should you talk to a doctor?

Talk to a doctor or GP if your sensitivities are frequent, disrupt your work or study, or you haven't had a proper diagnosis. A short appointment with your notes in hand makes the visit much more productive. Our guide on [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine) explains who to book and how to prepare.

Some situations are different. If light sensitivity comes with a sudden, severe headache, fever, stiff neck, confusion, weakness or a first-ever unusual symptom, seek urgent medical help. Our article on [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) lists the warning signs.

## Key takeaways

Your sensitivity to light, noise and smells has a reason: during migraine, the brain handles sensory input differently, and the effect can last beyond the headache itself. It doesn't mean you're fragile or overreacting.

Noting which senses react, when, and how strongly gives you a clearer map of your attacks over time. Bring that map to your doctor, and you'll both have more to work with.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why am I so sensitive to light during a migraine?

During an attack, light signals from the eyes reach brain areas that are also involved in migraine pain, so brightness can feel uncomfortable or make the pain worse. Research on people with migraine who are blind found that [those with no light perception reported no photophobia](https://content.news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2010/01/light-worsens-migraine-headaches). This suggests the signal travels from the retina to the brain.

### Is sensitivity to light and sound always a sign of migraine?

No. It's a common migraine feature, but it isn't proof on its own. A doctor looks at the full picture, including the pain's character, duration and other symptoms, before diagnosing migraine.

### Can smell sensitivity trigger a migraine?

Possibly. [About half of people with migraine report odor-triggered attacks](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4038337), but a smell can also just feel unbearable once an attack has already started. Tracking when the smell bothered you versus when the pain began can help you tell the difference.

### Do light and sound sensitivity go away between attacks?

Often they ease, but not always. Studies show people with migraine tend to be [more sensitive to sound and visual stimuli than people without migraine, even between attacks](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4038337).

### Do tinted glasses help with migraine light sensitivity?

They may help some people, but the evidence is limited. Ask your doctor or an eye care professional about options before buying, since not all tinted lenses are the same.
