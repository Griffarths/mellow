You have been logging your attacks for a few weeks. You have a calendar full of dots, a handful of numbers, and a vague feeling that Mondays are bad. Now what? The answer lies in your migraine diary patterns, and learning to read them takes a little method.

Spotting **migraine diary patterns** is a different skill from filling in the diary. This article walks you through how to read your calendar, your attack length and your intensity scores, and how to tell a real trend from a coincidence. It is part of our [Living with migraine](/blog/living-with-migraine) guides, and it picks up where "what to write down" leaves off.

**In short**

- A migraine diary only becomes useful once you review it, ideally after at least [three months of entries](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/self-management/keeping-a-migraine-diary/).
- Start with the big picture (how many headache days per month), then look at timing, length and intensity.
- A pattern deserves your attention when it repeats several times. One bad week proves very little.
- Some "triggers" may actually be early signs of the attack, so read cause and effect with caution, and bring your findings to a doctor.

## Why reading your diary matters as much as filling it in

Writing things down is only half the job, because **the value comes from looking back at the whole picture**. According to [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20360207), a headache diary can help you learn what triggers your migraines and which treatment works best. It can also help your healthcare professional diagnose your condition and track your progress between visits.

There is a second reason that is easy to miss. Memory is unreliable with pain. [The American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/headache-journals/) notes that many people underestimate how many days they have a headache, because they count the really bad days and forget the milder ones.

Your diary corrects that. It gives you numbers instead of impressions, which is what you will want in front of you at an appointment.

## What data do you need before you start analysing?

You need **a few weeks of simple, honest entries, not a perfect record**. The [Migraine Trust](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/self-management/keeping-a-migraine-diary/) suggests keeping it simple: the date, the day of the week, how long the attack lasted, and how severe it was. Severity can be mild, moderate or severe, or a score from 1 to 10.

If you are not sure what else to add, our guide on [what to write down in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) covers symptoms, medication and possible triggers.

Two practical points before you dive in:

- **Give it time.** The Migraine Trust says three months or more helps you and your doctor work out what your triggers might be. One month is a start, but it is a small sample.
- **Count the mild days too.** If you only log the big attacks, your totals will look better than reality, and your doctor will get a skewed picture.

## How to read your migraine calendar

Start with the **monthly count**, then zoom in on timing. Frequency matters a lot: the American Migraine Foundation says it is [very important when choosing treatment options](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/headache-journals/), so count every headache day, from mild to severe.

For context, the same source describes episodic migraine as 14 or fewer headache days per month. [Chronic migraine](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/what-is-chronic-migraine/) is defined as headaches on at least 15 days per month, with at least eight of those meeting migraine criteria. These labels are for doctors, not for you to self-diagnose. If your count is creeping up, it is simply a good reason to book an appointment (see [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine)).

Once you have the monthly total, look at the calendar like a map:

- **Days of the week.** Do attacks cluster on certain days, for instance after a stressful week or when you finally slow down? Our article on [weekend migraines](/blog/weekend-migraines) explains why that can happen.
- **Time of day.** Do you wake up with it, or does it build in the afternoon?
- **Position in the month.** Do attacks bunch up around the same point in your cycle?
- **Gaps and streaks.** Are there long quiet stretches, or several attacks close together?

Don't try to find everything at once. Pick one question per review, such as "Which days of the week?", and look only at that.

### What about your cycle?

If you menstruate, mark the first day of each period in the same calendar. According to the [Migraine Trust](https://migrainetrust.org/understand-migraine/types-of-migraine/menstrual-migraine/), the most accurate way to find out whether your attacks are linked to your periods is to keep a diary for at least three months, recording both attacks and period days.

Menstrual migraine attacks tend to fall from around two days before a period starts up to the third day of bleeding, and affect at least two out of three periods. If that sounds familiar, read [migraine and periods](/blog/migraine-and-periods) and talk to your doctor about what you see.

## What do attack length and intensity tell you?

Duration and intensity show you **how your attacks behave over time**, not just how often they come. Both are worth plotting, even roughly.

### Duration

Typical numbers give you a reference point. The American Migraine Foundation says the headache phase [can last from four hours to three days](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/how-long-does-a-migraine-attack-last/). The whole attack, including the warning and recovery phases, usually lasts one to two days. Our guide to [how long a migraine lasts](/blog/how-long-does-a-migraine-last) goes through each phase.

When you review your diary, ask:

- Is your average length stable, getting shorter or getting longer?
- Do attacks that you treat early end sooner than the ones you wait out? (This is something to discuss with your doctor, not to decide alone.)
- Have any run past 72 hours? The same foundation says that is a moment to [talk to your provider about a plan](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/how-long-does-a-migraine-attack-last/). You can also read [migraine that won't go away](/blog/migraine-that-wont-go-away).

If you log the hangover day afterwards separately, you will see the real cost of an attack. Our piece on [migraine postdrome](/blog/migraine-hangover-postdrome) explains why you can feel wiped out even when the pain is gone.

### Intensity

A 1 to 10 scale works well if you stay consistent. The point is not that your 7 matches anyone else's 7. The point is that your 7 means the same thing in March and in June.

Look for changes in the **average score** and in the **number of severe attacks**, rather than staring at one terrible day. A month with more attacks but lower scores tells a different story from a month with fewer, harsher ones. Your doctor will find both useful.

### Medication days

If you note when you take acute medication, count those days too. The American Migraine Foundation explains that [taking acute medication too often can itself lead to more headaches](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/what-is-medication-overuse-headache/). It also says the days from different medications add up together, so alternating between two will not reset the count.

The thresholds differ by type of medication, which is a good reason to show your diary to your doctor or pharmacist rather than judge it yourself. For an overview of the main options, see [migraine medication explained](/blog/migraine-medication).

## How do you spot a real trend without jumping to conclusions?

A trend is **something that repeats across several attacks and several weeks**, not something that happened twice. A simple rule helps: treat any pattern as a hypothesis until you have seen it at least three or four times.

Here is a gentle way to test an idea:

1. **Write the guess down.** For example: "I get attacks the day after a short night of sleep."
2. **Check both directions.** How many attacks followed a short night? And how many short nights were *not* followed by an attack?
3. **Watch for a few more weeks.** If the link holds, it gets stronger. If it fades, you have learned something too.
4. **Change one thing at a time**, if you try adjusting anything, so you can tell what made the difference.

The second step is the one people skip. If you only look at the days that went wrong, almost everything looks like a trigger.

Remember that migraine rarely has a single cause. Our guide on [how to identify your migraine triggers](/blog/identify-migraine-triggers) explains why triggers often add up, and why the same thing may not set off an attack every time. The same goes for [barometric pressure](/blog/barometric-pressure-and-migraines): it is worth noting, but the science is nuanced, so look for repeated links rather than a one-off storm.

## Which traps should you avoid when reading your diary?

The main traps are **mixing up cause and effect, and drawing big conclusions from small samples**. Here are the common ones.

### Confusing a warning sign with a trigger

This one catches almost everyone. A [study on patient-perceived triggers](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8068686/) found that some triggers people report, such as light, sound or skipping meals, may actually be early symptoms of the attack itself.

Mayo Clinic notes that [one or two days before a migraine](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20360201), some people notice subtle changes such as food cravings, mood changes or neck stiffness. So if you ate chocolate and then got a migraine, the craving may have come first. Our article on [migraine prodrome symptoms](/blog/migraine-prodrome-symptoms) helps you recognise these early signs.

This does not mean triggers are imaginary. It means the diary alone can't always tell you which came first, so hold your conclusions loosely.

### Remembering only the dramatic days

We tend to blame the most memorable event: the glass of wine, the argument, the missed meal. Log every day, including the quiet ones, so your diary can show that the same thing happened on days with no attack.

### Reading too much into a small sample

A single bad month, a holiday, an illness or a stressful deadline can distort your numbers. Mark unusual periods with a short note, so you know to treat them differently.

### Changing five things at once

If you change your sleep, caffeine, exercise and meals all in the same fortnight, you won't know what helped. Pick one change, give it a few weeks, and keep logging.

### Letting the diary become a source of stress

If checking your numbers makes you anxious, shrink the routine. A short weekly look is enough. The goal is to understand your migraines, not to monitor yourself constantly.

## What should you do with what you find?

Bring it to a professional, and **use it to ask better questions** rather than to make treatment decisions on your own. The Migraine Trust describes the diary as a way to help your doctor make a diagnosis and [check whether your treatment is working](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/self-management/keeping-a-migraine-diary/).

Before an appointment, prepare a short summary:

- How many headache days and migraine days you had per month.
- Your typical attack length and intensity, and any change over time.
- The patterns you think you see, with the caveat that they are guesses.
- Which acute medications you took, and on how many days.

Never stop, change or add a medication because of what your diary seems to show. That is a conversation for your doctor. If you are not sure who to see, read [when to see a doctor for migraine](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-migraine). And if a headache is sudden and severe, or comes with fever, a stiff neck, confusion, double vision or weakness, [Mayo Clinic advises](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/migraine-headache/symptoms-causes/syc-20360201) seeking care right away. See also [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine).

## A simple review routine you can stick to

A light routine beats a perfect one. Here is a rhythm that most people can keep up:

- **Every week (5 minutes):** check that you logged every attack, including the mild ones. Add a note for anything unusual.
- **Every month (10 minutes):** count headache days and migraine days, note the average length and intensity, and ask one question of your calendar.
- **Every three months (15 minutes):** compare the months side by side. Is anything moving in a clear direction? Write down what you want to ask your doctor.

If you use an app, much of the counting is done for you, which leaves you free to do the interesting part: thinking about what it means.

## Conclusion: patience makes your diary useful

Analysing your migraine diary is mostly about patience. Start with the monthly count, add timing, length and intensity, and treat every pattern as a question until it has repeated. Stay curious about triggers, but remember that some may be early signs of the attack rather than the cause.

You don't need to crack the code alone. Your diary is a tool for the conversation with your doctor, and a few clear, honest months of data can make that conversation much more productive.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should I keep a migraine diary before looking for patterns?

The Migraine Trust suggests keeping it for three months or more to help work out possible triggers. You can do a quick check after a month, but treat anything you find as a first impression. For menstrual links, aim for at least three cycles.

### What counts as a pattern in a migraine diary?

A pattern is something that repeats across several attacks and several weeks, such as attacks clustering at the same point in your cycle or on the same day of the week. A single coincidence is not a pattern. It also helps to check how often the suspected factor happened without an attack.

### Is a 1 to 10 intensity scale accurate enough?

It is accurate enough if you use it consistently. The scale doesn't need to match anyone else's, only your own from one month to the next. If numbers feel awkward, mild, moderate and severe also work, as the Migraine Trust notes.

### Can my diary tell me what triggers my migraines?

It can give you strong clues, but not certainty. Research suggests some reported triggers may be early symptoms of an attack, so the order of events can be misleading. Your doctor can help you interpret what you see.

### Should I be worried if my headache days are increasing?

Not necessarily, but it is worth acting on. A rising count is a good reason to book an appointment and bring your diary, so a professional can look at the full picture. Seek urgent help if you have a sudden, severe headache or symptoms such as fever, stiff neck, confusion or weakness.
