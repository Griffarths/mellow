You wake up with a tight, achy neck and a headache building behind your eyes. Or the pain starts at the base of your skull and creeps forward. You wonder: is this a migraine, or is something wrong with my neck?

Migraine neck pain is very common, and in most cases the neck is **part of the migraine**, not the cause. A separate condition called cervicogenic headache does exist, where the pain really does come from the neck, but it is less common and needs a proper assessment. This article explains why your neck hurts during an attack, how a neck-origin headache differs, and how to talk to a doctor without trying to diagnose yourself. It sits alongside our guide to [migraine diagnosis](/blog/migraine-diagnosis), which covers the bigger picture.

**In short**

- Neck pain or stiffness is a very common part of a migraine attack, and it can even show up before the head pain starts.
- A cervicogenic headache is a different condition: head pain referred from a problem in the neck, often one-sided and made worse by neck movement.
- Only a doctor can tell the two apart, usually with a physical exam, and sometimes with imaging or a diagnostic nerve block.
- A stiff neck with a high temperature, a rash or a sudden, severe headache needs urgent medical help, not a wait-and-see approach.

## How common is migraine neck pain?

Very common. A [systematic review and meta-analysis](https://research.regionh.dk/da/publications/prevalence-of-neck-pain-in-migraine-a-systematic-review-and-meta-/) found that about 77% of people with migraine reported neck pain, compared with about 23% of people without headaches. It was even more frequent in chronic migraine (87%) than in episodic migraine (77%).

The American Migraine Foundation says neck pain is [much more frequent than nausea](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/neck-pain-migraine/) in migraine, and that it can be a good clue that an attack is starting. If you have ever thought "my neck is a mess, so it must be a neck problem", you are in good company. Many people with migraine do the same, simply because the neck is where they feel it first.

If you want a refresher on how attacks unfold, our article on [what causes migraines](/blog/what-causes-migraines) explains what happens in the brain.

## Why does your neck hurt during a migraine?

Your neck hurts during a migraine mostly because of how pain signals from the head and neck are wired together. Nerves from the upper neck and nerves from the face and head meet in the same relay area in the brainstem and upper spinal cord, called the [trigeminocervical complex](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fneur.2019.00276/full).

Because the signals share a junction, your brain can have trouble telling where the pain is coming from. Researchers describe this as pain being "referred" in both directions: a migraine can make the neck feel sore, and in some cases the neck itself may add to the headache. The exact balance is still not fully understood, and scientists openly say the reasons for neck pain in migraine remain unclear.

### Neck stiffness can be an early warning sign

For some people, the neck is the first thing to go. The American Migraine Foundation lists [stiff muscles, especially in the neck](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/migraine-prodrome-symptoms-prevention/), among the symptoms of the prodrome, the early phase of an attack that can start hours before the headache. Others include mood changes, tiredness, yawning and food cravings.

This matters because a tight neck in the morning is not always the cause of your headache later. It may be the first sign of the attack itself. Keeping notes can help you spot this pattern (more on that below).

### A symptom, not a trigger

It used to be common to think that neck tension triggered migraines. The American Migraine Foundation now explains that [neck pain is a symptom of migraine, not a cause](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/neck-pain-migraine/). Having migraine and neck pain together does not automatically mean something is wrong with your neck.

The same source notes the relationship can go both ways: treating the migraine may ease the neck pain, though not always in a direct or predictable way. That is a good question to bring to your doctor.

## What is a cervicogenic headache?

A cervicogenic headache is head pain that is referred from a problem in the neck, such as the joints, discs or soft tissues of the cervical spine (the top part of your spine). The [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/cervicogenic-headache) describes it as pain you feel in your head that originates from the bony structures or soft tissues in your neck, for example after an injury or because of arthritis or a slipped disc.

The term sounds technical, but the idea is simple. The pain is felt in the head, yet the source is somewhere in the neck. That is the opposite of a migraine, where the neck pain is a passenger and the attack starts in the brain.

Typical features described by the Cleveland Clinic include:

- Pain that starts in the neck and travels to the head or face.
- Pain on one side of the head.
- Limited movement in the neck.
- Tender spots in the neck.

You may also hear this called "cervical migraine" online. That is not a separate official type of migraine. If you are curious about the real forms of migraine, our [simple map of migraine types](/blog/types-of-migraine) lays them out clearly.

## Migraine or cervicogenic headache: what differences can you notice?

The clearest differences are what sets the pain off and which other symptoms come with it. These are clues to share with your doctor, not a way to diagnose yourself.

| | Migraine with neck pain | Cervicogenic headache |
|---|---|---|
| Where it starts | Often in the head, with the neck joining in | In the neck, then spreads to the head |
| Neck movement | Neck is sore, but movement is not usually the main trigger | Certain neck movements or pressure on spots in the neck can make it worse |
| Side of the head | Can be one side or both | Often one side only |
| Other symptoms | Light and sound sensitivity, nausea, sometimes aura | Typically without these |

This table reflects what the [American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/cervicogenic-headache/) and the [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/cervicogenic-headache) describe. For example, they note that cervicogenic headache often comes with a reduced range of motion in the neck, and that it usually does not bring the light sensitivity, sound sensitivity, nausea or vomiting that are common in migraine.

In real life, though, the line can blur. Cervicogenic headache is hard to diagnose because referred pain is easily mistaken for tension-type headache or migraine, according to the Cleveland Clinic. You can also have more than one kind of headache. If you want to understand how migraine compares with ordinary headaches, see [migraine vs headache](/blog/migraine-vs-headache).

## How do doctors tell the difference?

Doctors usually start with your story and a physical exam of your neck and head. The Cleveland Clinic says a clinical diagnosis based on your symptoms and a thorough exam can confirm cervicogenic headache without always needing other tests. Imaging such as an X-ray, CT scan or MRI may be used in some cases.

The American Migraine Foundation adds that anyone suspected of having cervicogenic headache should be [carefully assessed to exclude other causes](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/cervicogenic-headache/), including migraine and tension-type headache. In some cases, a nerve block is used both as a diagnostic tool and as a treatment: if numbing the neck structures stops the headache, that supports the diagnosis.

If a neck problem is confirmed, the same source says the first step is often physical therapy and an ongoing exercise plan, with other options depending on the person. What is right for you is a decision for your doctor or specialist, so please do not start or stop anything on your own.

## How can you talk to your doctor without self-diagnosing?

Describe what you feel, in your own words, and let the doctor draw the conclusions. You do not need to arrive with a label like "cervicogenic". A clear picture of your attacks is far more helpful than a guess.

Here are details that make an appointment more useful:

- **Where it starts:** neck first, head first, or both at once?
- **Which side:** always the same side, or does it switch?
- **What changes it:** does turning your head, a certain posture or pressing on your neck make it worse?
- **What else comes with it:** nausea, light or sound sensitivity, visual changes, dizziness.
- **Timing:** how long it lasts, how often it happens, and whether your neck feels stiff hours before the pain.
- **History:** any past neck injury, whiplash or long periods of desk work.

A few questions to ask:

- "Could my neck pain be part of my migraine, or does it look like a separate problem?"
- "Do I need an exam of my neck, or any tests?"
- "Would a physiotherapist or another specialist be useful?"

Our guide on [what to write in a migraine diary](/blog/migraine-diary-what-to-note) shows how to record this without turning it into homework. If your headaches are happening very often, [Why Do I Have a Headache Every Day?](/blog/headache-every-day) is worth reading before your visit.

## Stiff neck and headache: when to get urgent help

A headache with a stiff neck needs urgent care if it comes with a high temperature, a rash, confusion or a sudden, severe onset. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/headaches/) advises calling 999 or going to A&E if you have a headache together with a stiff neck, a very high temperature, or bright lights bothering you. In the US and some other countries, the equivalent is calling emergency services (911) or going to the ER.

The [NHS also lists](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/meningitis/) headache, a stiff neck, dislike of bright lights, a high temperature, vomiting, confusion and a rash that does not fade when a glass is rolled over it as possible signs of meningitis, a serious infection that needs treatment straight away. The NHS also says to get urgent help for a headache that starts suddenly and is extremely painful, or if you have confusion, drowsiness or loss of vision.

This is not meant to alarm you. Most stiff necks with a migraine are just that. But the combination above is different from your usual pattern, and it is always better to be checked. Our article on [when to go to the ER for a migraine](/blog/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-migraine) goes through these warning signs in more detail.

## In practice: caring for a sore neck without guilt

A sore neck during migraine is not your fault, and you do not need to "fix your posture" to deserve relief. Nothing here replaces medical advice, but a few gentle habits can make attacks easier to live with.

- **Notice the early signs.** If neck stiffness often arrives before the headache, jot it down. Spotting your own early warning can give you and your doctor useful information.
- **Take breaks from long stretches in one position.** Moving a little during long screen or desk sessions is kind to your body, though research has not shown that it prevents migraine.
- **Try comfort measures during an attack.** Rest in a quiet, dim room and look at our ideas to [relieve a migraine without medication](/blog/relieve-migraine-without-medication).
- **Ask before you try manipulation.** If you are thinking about physiotherapy or other hands-on treatments for your neck, mention your migraine and check with your doctor first.

## Conclusion

Migraine neck pain is real, common and usually part of the attack, not proof that your neck is "the problem". A cervicogenic headache is a separate condition, where the pain starts in the neck, often on one side, and gets worse with certain movements. Only a doctor can tell which one you have, or whether it is both.

So instead of guessing, observe. Note where the pain starts, what makes it worse and what comes with it, then share those details at your appointment. And if a stiff neck arrives with a high fever, a rash or a sudden, severe headache, get urgent help.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can migraine cause neck pain and stiffness?

Yes. A meta-analysis found neck pain in about 77% of people with migraine, compared with about 23% of people without headaches. The American Migraine Foundation describes neck pain as a symptom of migraine, and it can appear before the head pain begins.

### Is "cervical migraine" a real diagnosis?

Not as a separate type of migraine. The term is often used online to describe head pain linked to the neck, which doctors usually call cervicogenic headache. If you think this fits you, ask your doctor to assess your symptoms rather than relying on the label.

### How do I know if my headache comes from my neck?

You cannot know for sure on your own. Clues described by the Cleveland Clinic include pain that starts in the neck, one-sided pain, limited neck movement and tender spots, but these overlap with other headaches. A doctor can examine your neck and rule out other causes.

### When is a stiff neck with a headache an emergency?

According to the NHS, a headache with a stiff neck and a very high temperature, or with bright lights bothering you, calls for emergency help (999 in the UK). A rash that does not fade under a glass, confusion, or a sudden and extremely painful headache are also reasons to seek urgent care.
